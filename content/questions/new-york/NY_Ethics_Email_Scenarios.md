# LawScape New York Ethics Email Pack

## Instructions

Assume you are an associate at Van Rensselaer, Okafor & Delacroix LLP, a fictional New York firm with offices in Manhattan, Brooklyn, Albany, Buffalo and a seasonal outpost in Lake Placid. Each email below comes from a partner, a client or a colleague. Select the single best response under the **New York Rules of Professional Conduct (22 NYCRR Part 1200)** and, where indicated, the Joint Rules of the Appellate Divisions (22 NYCRR Part 1215).

These 28 questions are original educational hypotheticals for fictional clients. They are unofficial New York practice, not official bar-exam questions or legal advice. The game's office remains Hardsell & Firestone; this pack's visiting fictional firm provides the emails.

Source check: September 27, 2026, using the rule text republished through July 1, 2026 and relevant court amendment orders. NYSBA comments are not court-adopted rules and are not answer authority. Publication was authorized by the project owner; no independent New York practitioner sign-off is recorded. See [source review and corrections](SOURCES.md).

## 1. The Brownstone Down Payment

> **From:** Chidi Okafor, Brooklyn Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Park Slope brownstone - where does the deposit go?
>
> Good morning from the Fourth Avenue F train, which is running local again. We represent the seller of a Park Slope brownstone. The buyer's ten percent contract deposit, $215,000, arrives by wire this afternoon and we hold it as escrowee until closing in sixty days.
>
> Our bookkeeper says the operating account has the best interest rate and closing is only two months out, so she proposes to park the deposit there and move it at closing. The buyer's counsel also mentioned a bank in New Jersey the buyer prefers. Which account do we use?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** The operating account is acceptable for a short escrow period as long as the firm keeps a ledger showing the buyer's share.
- **B.** Any bank the buyer prefers will do, in or out of New York, because the funds belong to the buyer.
- **C.** Deposit it into the firm's Attorney Escrow Account at a New York banking institution complying with Part 1300 dishonored-check and overdraft reporting; the account may not have overdraft protection, and it may be moved to an out-of-state bank only if that bank also complies with Part 1300 and the owner gives prior written approval specifying the branch's name and address.
- **D.** Open a personal savings account in the associate's name earning interest for the buyer, since the associate is not a party to the sale.

## 2. The Court of Appeals Case on the Ferry

> **From:** Luis Ortega, Staten Island Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Found a bad case on the 7:20 ferry
>
> Reading advance sheets on the ferry this morning, I found a Court of Appeals decision squarely against our position on the motion to dismiss in the St. George warehouse case. It is directly on point and it is controlling. Opposing counsel's papers do not cite it and their reply is already filed.
>
> The client says the other side had every chance to find it and we should say nothing. I think we can distinguish it on the lease language. What goes in our sur-reply?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Omit the decision because the duty to disclose adverse authority applies only to statutes, not case law.
- **B.** Disclose the decision to the court and argue that it is distinguishable on the lease language.
- **C.** Omit the decision because it is opposing counsel's burden to find its own authority and the client has instructed silence.
- **D.** Withdraw from the motion rather than cite a case against the client.

## 3. The Winery Owner's Text Message

> **From:** Yuki Tanabe, Ithaca Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Seneca Lake winery dispute - can I text her directly?
>
> We represent one co-owner of a Seneca Lake winery in a buyout dispute. The other co-owner has retained counsel in Rochester. Our client is frustrated that the lawyers are slowing everything down and says the two owners could settle this over a glass of Riesling in an afternoon.
>
> The other owner texted our client last night suggesting the same thing. Can I text her a term sheet myself, and can our client sit down with her?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** You may text her a term sheet as long as you copy her lawyer afterward.
- **B.** Neither you nor the client may communicate with her about the dispute while she is represented.
- **C.** You may communicate with her directly because she initiated contact with our client.
- **D.** You may not communicate with her without her lawyer's prior consent, but the client may meet her and you may counsel him about that meeting, provided you give her lawyer reasonable advance notice that the conversation will take place.

## 4. The Great Camp in the Will

> **From:** Bea Underhill, Lake Placid Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Mrs. Halvorsen wants to leave me the camp on Upper Saranac
>
> Mrs. Halvorsen, our longtime estate-planning client, came in after her swim and told me she wants to update her will to leave me the family's Great Camp on Upper Saranac Lake. She has no children and says I am the only person who has ever cared about the boathouse. I did not suggest it. We are not related.
>
> She asked me to draft the codicil this week. Can I?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. You may not prepare an instrument giving yourself a gift because you are not related to her; she should be referred to independent counsel if she wishes to proceed.
- **B.** Yes, because she raised the gift on her own and the Rules prohibit only solicited gifts.
- **C.** Yes, provided another partner in the firm drafts the codicil instead of you.
- **D.** Yes, provided the gift is disclosed to the Surrogate's Court when the will is probated.

## 5. The Orchard's Nonrefundable Retainer

> **From:** Nadia Petrov, Hudson Valley Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** New Paltz orchard - retainer language
>
> A Hudson Valley apple orchard wants to retain us for a water-rights dispute with the neighboring cidery. Cash flow is seasonal for them, so the owner offered a $20,000 "nonrefundable retainer, earned upon receipt" as advance payment for our work on the dispute before the harvest, not solely for availability.
>
> I like the certainty. Can the engagement letter say that?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A nonrefundable retainer is permitted whenever the client proposes it and is sophisticated.
- **B.** No. New York prohibits nonrefundable retainer fees, but the letter may include a reasonable minimum fee clause if it defines in plain language the circumstances under which the minimum is incurred and how it is calculated.
- **C.** Yes, provided the retainer is deposited in the escrow account rather than the operating account.
- **D.** No. New York prohibits any advance payment of fees before services are rendered.

## 6. The Rochester Referral

> **From:** Hannah Weisz, Rochester Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Garbage plate lunch and a referral fee
>
> A solo practitioner I know from the Monroe County bar sent us a product-liability case over lunch at the diner across from the Public Market. She will do no work on the file but wants one-third of our contingent fee as a referral fee.
>
> The client has not been told about any of this. Can we agree to it?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A pure referral fee to another lawyer is permitted without client involvement.
- **B.** No. New York prohibits any division of a fee with a lawyer outside the firm.
- **C.** Yes, provided the referring lawyer is paid from the firm's operating account rather than from the client's recovery.
- **D.** Only if the division is in proportion to services performed or, by a writing given to the client, she assumes joint responsibility; the client agrees after full disclosure including each lawyer's share, confirmed in writing; and the total fee is not excessive.

## 7. The Saratoga Syndicate

> **From:** Colette Abernathy, Saratoga Springs Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Two partners, one racehorse, one lawyer?
>
> Two friends who co-own a thoroughbred stabled at Saratoga want us to represent both of them in negotiating a sale of the horse to a Kentucky buyer. Their interests mostly align, but they have different views on how the proceeds should be split and one of them has a side arrangement with the trainer that the other does not know about.
>
> They are both fine with us doing the deal and told me so over coffee at the track. Can we proceed on that basis?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Their oral agreement at the track is sufficient because the representation is transactional rather than litigation.
- **B.** No. A lawyer may never represent two clients on the same side of a transaction.
- **C.** Only if you reasonably believe you can represent each competently and diligently, the representation is not prohibited by law, and each client gives informed consent confirmed in writing; on these facts the undisclosed side arrangement may make the conflict nonconsentable.
- **D.** Yes, provided the firm charges each owner a separate fee.

## 8. The Stadium Vendor Comes Back Around

> **From:** Sal Petracca, Bronx Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Former client on the other side of the concession contract
>
> Three years ago we represented a Bronx food vendor in negotiating a concession contract near the stadium on River Avenue. The engagement ended after the deal closed. The concession operator has now asked us to represent it in terminating that same contract for breach.
>
> The handling lawyer has retired, but I have accessed confidential information in the old file material to this dispute. Are we clear?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. This is the same matter and the operator's interests are materially adverse to the former client, so the firm may proceed only if the former client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.
- **B.** Yes. The duty to a former client ends when the engagement ends and the file goes to storage.
- **C.** Yes, provided the retired lawyer does not consult on the new matter.
- **D.** Yes, because the former client has not paid for any work in three years and is no longer a client.

## 9. The Lateral from Buffalo

> **From:** Declan Foyle, Buffalo Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** New lateral hire - can we wall her off from the Erie County litigation?
>
> We are about to hire a litigator from a Buffalo firm that is opposing us in the pending Supreme Court, Erie County, case over the waterfront development. She was the day-to-day lead on that case for the other side for two years: she ran the discovery, staffed the depositions and made the strategic calls.
>
> I assume we screen her, pay her no part of the fee, and notify the other side in writing, then carry on. Correct?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Correct. A timely screen with notice and no fee share always removes imputation in New York.
- **B.** No. Because the matter is litigation and she substantially participated in its management and direction with day-to-day decision-making responsibility, a screen will not prevent imputation; any waiver must meet Rule 1.7's conditions, including the affected clients' informed consent confirmed in writing; without that, the firm cannot continue the adverse representation after hiring her.
- **C.** Correct, provided she also signs an affidavit that she will not discuss the case.
- **D.** No. A firm may never hire a lawyer from opposing counsel's firm while a matter is pending.

## 10. The Restaurant Owner Who Walked Out

> **From:** Sal Petracca, Bronx Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Arthur Avenue consultation - the landlord now wants us
>
> Two weeks ago I spent forty minutes with a restaurant owner on Arthur Avenue who was thinking of suing his landlord over a lease dispute. He told me quite a lot about his finances and his negotiating bottom line. He decided not to hire us.
>
> This morning the landlord called and wants to retain the firm in the same dispute. I know things that would hurt the restaurant owner. What are our options?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** The firm may take the landlord freely because the restaurant owner never became a client.
- **B.** The firm may take the landlord as long as you personally do not work on it, without further steps.
- **C.** The firm may never represent the landlord.
- **D.** You are personally disqualified; the firm may proceed only if both the landlord and the restaurant owner give informed consent confirmed in writing, or if you took reasonable measures to limit the information you received, the firm promptly gives appropriate internal notice, implements an effective screen with no fee share, and promptly gives the restaurant owner written notice (subject to the rule's confidentiality postponement); in either route a reasonable lawyer must conclude the firm can represent competently and diligently.

## 11. Leaving the Kings County Case

> **From:** Chidi Okafor, Brooklyn Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Client stopped paying - can I just stop showing up?
>
> Our client in the Supreme Court, Kings County, commercial case on Court Street deliberately disregards his fee agreement despite three invoices and our calls. Assume the applicable court rules require permission to withdraw. Trial is in six weeks. I want out. I plan to send a letter saying we are done, keep the unused portion of the $25,000 advance to cover the time we spent chasing him, and hold the file until he pays.
>
> Anything wrong with that plan?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Nothing. Nonpayment is grounds for withdrawal and the file may be held until the bill is paid.
- **B.** You must continue through trial because withdrawal is never permitted within sixty days of trial.
- **C.** Nonpayment may permit withdrawal, but because the applicable court rules require it, you need permission to withdraw, must continue if the court orders you to, and on termination must give reasonable notice, deliver the papers the client is entitled to, and promptly refund any unearned part of the advance.
- **D.** You may withdraw by letter, but must return the entire advance including the earned portion.

## 12. The Salt Potato Engagement Letter

> **From:** Tomasz Kowalczyk, Syracuse Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Quick zoning matter for the Tipperary Hill client
>
> A Tipp Hill tavern owner wants us to handle a zoning variance before the city board. We expect the fee to be roughly $7,500 on an hourly basis. We have never represented him before. He is in a hurry and says a handshake is fine; he trusts us because we are on the same side of the green-over-red traffic light.
>
> Do we need paper before we start?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Because the expected fee exceeds $3,000 and this is a new client, Part 1215 requires a written letter of engagement or retainer agreement stating the scope of services, the fee and expense basis and billing practices, and, where applicable, notice of the right to fee arbitration under Part 137. The letter must precede starting, with a reasonable-time delay only if earlier delivery is impracticable or the scope cannot yet be determined; a signed retainer covering the required matters is an alternative under section 1215.1(c).
- **B.** No. A written engagement letter is required only for contingent-fee and domestic-relations matters.
- **C.** No. A handshake is sufficient if the client waives the writing.
- **D.** Yes, but the letter may be provided at the end of the matter along with the final bill.

## 13. The Alexandria Bay Boat Check

> **From:** Margarethe Van Rensselaer, Albany Office Managing Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Escrow disbursement for the Thousand Islands closing
>
> We are closing a boat sale in Alexandria Bay on Friday and need to disburse $40,000 from escrow to the seller, who prefers cash. Our Watertown office manager, who is not a lawyer, has been added as a signatory on the escrow account so he can cut the check while I am up in the islands.

> Fine to write the check "to cash" and have him sign it?

- **C.** Yes, because the office manager is a firm employee acting under your supervision.
- **D.** No, because escrow funds may be disbursed only by court order.

## 14. The Metro-North Platform Incident

> **From:** Nadia Petrov, Hudson Valley Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Insurer wants us to reach out to the injured passengers now
>
> A passenger was seriously injured on a station platform in Westchester six days ago. We represent the commuter-rail contractor's liability insurer, which will defend and indemnify the contractor. The claims adjuster wants us to write to the injured passenger and her family this week with a sympathetic letter and an offer to "talk things through."
>
> Nobody has filed anything yet. Can we send it?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The 30-day rule applies only to lawyers seeking to represent injured persons, not to defense counsel.
- **B.** Yes, provided the letter does not mention settlement.
- **C.** No. A lawyer representing an actual or potential defendant or its insurer may not make an unsolicited communication to the injured individual or her family before the 30th day after the incident, unless a filing must be made within 30 days as a legal prerequisite, in which case the 15th day applies.
- **D.** No. Defense counsel may never communicate with an injured person before suit is filed.

## 15. The Borscht Belt Barn

> **From:** Abe Rosenblatt, Catskills Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Client says he will burn the old resort barn for the insurance
>
> Our client owns a shuttered Catskills resort near Monticello. During a meeting about the property's insurance renewal he said, quite seriously, that if the buyer walks he will "have a little fire" in the barn next month and collect on the policy. I told him that is arson and insurance fraud and that we will not help. He laughed and changed the subject.
>
> Must I call the sheriff? May I? Or is this locked in the privilege?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** You may, but are not required to, reveal confidential information to the extent you reasonably believe necessary to prevent the client from committing the crime; you must not assist him, and you should continue to counsel him against it.
- **B.** You must report him to law enforcement immediately because the Rules mandate disclosure of any intended crime.
- **C.** You may not reveal anything because a client's stated intention to commit a crime is protected confidential information.
- **D.** You may reveal everything you know about the client's finances and the property to the insurer so it can cancel the policy.

## 16. The Cooperstown Dinner Story

> **From:** Rosalind Delacroix, Manhattan Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Great anecdote for the Hall of Fame weekend dinner

> I am speaking at a bar association dinner in Cooperstown during induction weekend. I want to tell the story of a former client, a well-known collectibles dealer, whose embarrassing bookkeeping habits we discovered while defending him in an Otsego County case five years ago. - **B.** Yes, provided you do not say the client's name.
- **C.** Yes, because the information was never privileged; it was just embarrassing.
- **D.** No. Information gained during the representation that would be embarrassing or detrimental to the former client remains confidential after the representation ends, and you may not reveal it unless the Rules permit or it has become generally known.

## 17. The 60 Centre Street Surprise

> **From:** Rosalind Delacroix, Manhattan Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Our witness lied on the stand at 60 Centre Street
>
> In the Supreme Court, New York County, trial this week our client testified that he never received the March notice of default. Over lunch on the courthouse steps he told me, with a grin, that of course he got it and shredded it. His testimony on that point is material.
>
> He forbids me to say anything and reminds me that what he told me is confidential. What are my obligations?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Do nothing, because the client's admission to you is confidential information and confidentiality overrides candor to the tribunal.
- **B.** Take reasonable remedial measures, beginning with urging the client to correct the testimony, and if that fails, disclose to the tribunal as necessary, even though the information is otherwise protected by Rule 1.6.
- **C.** Move to withdraw quietly and let successor counsel decide what to do.
- **D.** Ask the judge to strike the entire testimony without explaining why.

## 18. The Beef on Weck Witness

> **From:** Declan Foyle, Buffalo Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Paying the key witness - how much and when?
>
> The key fact witness in our Buffalo construction case is a retired ironworker who lives near Larkinville. He is willing to testify but will lose two days of wages and has to drive in from Lackawanna. Our client wants to "make it worth his while" and proposes $500 a day plus a $5,000 bonus if we win.
>
> What can we pay him?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Nothing. Any payment to a fact witness is prohibited.
- **B.** Both the daily amount and the bonus, as long as the amounts are disclosed to the court.
- **C.** Reasonable compensation for his loss of time in attending, testifying and preparing, plus reasonable related expenses; no payment contingent on the content of his testimony or the outcome of the case.
- **D.** Only his mileage, because fact witnesses may be reimbursed for expenses but never for lost time.

## 19. The Contractor's "Little Reminder"

> **From:** Sal Petracca, Bronx Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Leverage in the Pelham Bay renovation dispute
>
> Our client's contractor walked off a Pelham Bay renovation with a $60,000 deposit. We are demanding the money back. The client has learned the contractor may have skipped some sales-tax filings and wants our demand letter to say that unless the deposit is returned by Friday we will refer the tax issue to the District Attorney.
>
> It is true, and it would get his attention. Can we put it in the letter?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A lawyer may not present, participate in presenting, or threaten to present criminal charges solely to obtain an advantage in a civil matter.
- **B.** Yes, because the underlying allegation is true and truthful statements are always permitted.
- **C.** Yes, provided the letter is sent to the contractor's lawyer rather than to the contractor directly.
- **D.** Yes, because the threat concerns a matter related to the dispute.

## 20. The Judge at the Rail

> **From:** Colette Abernathy, Saratoga Springs Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Ran into the judge at the paddock
>
> I saw the judge assigned to our pending Saratoga County commercial case at the paddock before the fourth race. We chatted about the weather. She is deciding our summary judgment motion next week and I badly want to mention one point the other side glossed over.
>
> I would keep it to two sentences. Harmless?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Brief social conversation with a judge is permitted and a passing mention of the case is harmless.
- **B.** Yes, provided you send opposing counsel an email afterward summarizing what you said.
- **C.** No, and you should also report the judge for speaking with you.
- **D.** No. In an adversarial proceeding you may not communicate about the merits with the judge before whom the matter is pending except in official proceedings, in writing with a copy promptly delivered to all other parties, or orally on adequate notice to them, or as otherwise authorized by law.

## 21. The Polygraph and the Tabloid

> **From:** Marcus Greene, Long Island Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Reporter is calling about the Montauk marina case
>
> Our client is charged in Suffolk County with felony arson at a Montauk marina. He faces state prison time. A tabloid reporter wants a comment. The client passed a private polygraph last week and wants me to tell the reporter that, plus that the co-defendant "already confessed and named someone else."
>
> Can I say that?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may say anything about a criminal case that is true.
- **B.** No. In a criminal matter that could result in incarceration, statements about the results of any examination or test and about the existence or contents of a confession are ordinarily likely to prejudice the proceeding; subject to the no-material-prejudice requirement, you may state without elaboration the charge, that an investigation is ongoing, information in the public record, scheduling, and that the charge is an accusation and the client is presumed innocent.
- **C.** No. A criminal defense lawyer may never speak to the press while charges are pending.
- **D.** Yes, provided the statement is made on background and not for attribution.

## 22. The Coney Island Rent

> **From:** Chidi Okafor, Brooklyn Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Boardwalk client cannot make rent while the case is pending
>
> Our personal-injury client was hurt on a Coney Island ride and cannot work. We represent her on a contingent fee. She is two months behind on rent and asked whether the firm can front her $3,000 for rent and a MetroCard until the case settles. She would repay us from the recovery. I feel terrible for her.
>
> Can we do it?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may advance living expenses to a client in litigation if repayment is contingent on the outcome.
- **B.** Yes, provided the advance is documented as a loan at a fair interest rate.
- **C.** No. While representing a client in contemplated or pending litigation, our for-profit firm in this contingent-fee representation cannot advance her rent or living expenses; the applicable exceptions concern court costs and litigation expenses, although you may tell her about legal-aid and public assistance resources.
- **D.** No, unless the client signs a written waiver of the conflict.

## 23. The Astoria Tenant Across the Table

> **From:** Priya Raman, Queens Office Partner

- **C.** Yes, because the tenant may waive her right to counsel by signing.
- **D.** No. A lawyer may never communicate with an unrepresented adverse party.

## 24. The Misdirected Albany Email

> **From:** Margarethe Van Rensselaer, Albany Office Managing Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Opposing counsel just emailed us their internal strategy memo
>
> Opposing counsel in the Albany County Article 78 proceeding meant to forward a privileged strategy memo to her client and sent it to me instead. It is clearly marked privileged and clearly not meant for us. It also happens to be very useful.
>
> What does the Rule require me to do?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Nothing. A lawyer who receives an adversary's document by mistake may use it freely.
- **B.** File the memo with the court so the judge can decide whether it is privileged.
- **C.** Forward it to the client so the client can decide whether to use it.
- **D.** Promptly notify the sender that you received it; the Rule requires prompt notice when you know or reasonably should know a document was inadvertently sent.

## 25. The Hospital Waiting Room Plan

> **From:** Declan Foyle, Buffalo Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Marketing director's idea for the bus-crash cases
>
> After the tour-bus crash on the Thruway, our marketing director, who is not a lawyer, proposes to visit the hospital waiting rooms in Buffalo, hand out our brochures to injured passengers and their families, and sign them up on the spot. The passengers are strangers to us, are not lawyers, and do not routinely buy these services for business purposes. He says he, not a lawyer, will do the talking so the firm is safe.
>
> Green light?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The solicitation rules apply only to lawyers, so a nonlawyer employee may make the contact.
- **B.** No. Live person-to-person solicitation of strangers for pecuniary gain is prohibited, and a lawyer may not do through a nonlawyer what the lawyer may not do directly; a partner who orders or ratifies it is responsible.
- **C.** Yes, provided the brochures include the firm's name and address.
- **D.** Yes, provided the marketing director waits until the passengers are discharged.

## 26. The Disbarred Friend in Ithaca

> **From:** Yuki Tanabe, Ithaca Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Old classmate lost his license - can he "consult" for us?
>
> A law-school classmate of mine was disbarred last year. He is brilliant on Tompkins County land-use matters. He proposes to work for us as a "consultant": meeting our clients alone at the gorge-side cafe, advising them on their options, and drafting the papers we would file under my name.
>
> He would not appear in court. Is that a workable arrangement?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A disbarred lawyer may practice as a consultant as long as an admitted lawyer signs the filings.
- **B.** Yes, provided clients are told he is disbarred.
- **C.** No. A lawyer may not aid a nonlawyer in the unauthorized practice of law; independently advising clients on their legal options is practicing law, and you would also be responsible for his conduct as a nonlawyer under your supervision.
- **D.** No, unless the Appellate Division approves the consulting arrangement in advance.

## 27. The Prior Lawyer's Escrow

> **From:** Marcus Greene, Long Island Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Client says her old lawyer stole from escrow
>
> Our new client, a Hamptons landscaper, told me in confidence that the lawyer who handled her prior real-estate closing kept $30,000 of her escrow deposit and has been dodging her calls. She showed me the closing statement and the bank records. It is about as clear as these things get.
>
> She does not want to "make trouble" and asked me not to tell anyone. Do I have to report him?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Rule 8.3 requires a lawyer who knows of another lawyer's violation raising a substantial question about honesty to report it, but it does not require disclosure of information protected by Rule 1.6; you should explain the situation to the client and seek her informed consent to report, and if she refuses, the Rule does not require you to report.
- **B.** You must report him immediately regardless of the client's wishes because escrow theft is always reportable.
- **C.** You may never report because everything a client tells you is privileged.
- **D.** You must report him only if he is admitted in the same Judicial Department as you.

## 28. The Holiday Party at the Rink

> **From:** Rosalind Delacroix, Manhattan Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** What a partner said at the holiday party
>
> At the firm holiday party at the Rockefeller Center rink, a senior partner made repeated demeaning remarks to a junior associate about her national origin and her pregnancy, loudly enough for a group to hear. She was visibly upset. Another partner told me later that it was "just a party" and "not the practice of law," so the Rules do not apply.
>
> Is that right?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The Rules govern only conduct in court and in client matters.
- **B.** Yes, because the remarks, while unkind, were not unlawful.
- **C.** No, but only the associate can raise it and the firm has no obligation to act.
- **D.** No. Conduct in the practice of law includes interacting with coworkers and operating or managing a law firm, and harassment on the basis of a protected category is misconduct whether or not it is unlawful; partners with management responsibility must take reasonable remedial action.
