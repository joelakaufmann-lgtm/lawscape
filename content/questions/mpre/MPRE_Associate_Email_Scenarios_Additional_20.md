# MPRE Associate Email Scenarios: 20 Additional Questions

## Instructions

Assume you are a law-firm associate or government lawyer receiving each email below. Select the best response under the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct or, where indicated, the ABA Model Code of Judicial Conduct.

These are original educational hypotheticals, not official NCBE questions. State rules may differ from the ABA models.

---

## 16. Investing in the Client's Startup

> **From:** Maya Franklin, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Equity purchase from NovaGrid
>
> NovaGrid, a current firm client, offered me an opportunity to buy shares directly from the company. The price and terms are fair. I fully described the transaction and my role in a document the CEO understood. I orally told the CEO that independent legal advice would be a good idea and gave her two weeks to obtain it. She declined and signed a written consent to the essential terms and to my role in the deal.
>
> I did not put my advice about consulting independent counsel in writing. May I complete the purchase?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Fair terms and the client's signed consent are sufficient.
- **B.** Yes. A sophisticated business client does not need to be advised to obtain independent counsel.
- **C.** No. The lawyer must advise the client in writing of the desirability of independent counsel, in addition to satisfying the other safeguards.
- **D.** No. A lawyer may never enter a business transaction with a current client.

## 17. A Parent Paying the Defense Fees

> **From:** Leo Martinez, Client  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** My father will pay the bill
>
> My father has offered to pay your firm's fees for my criminal defense. I consent to that arrangement, but I want to remain the client and make all decisions about my case. Please do not give him confidential information or let him direct the strategy.
>
> Can the firm accept payment on those terms?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A lawyer may accept compensation only from the person being represented.
- **B.** Yes, if the client gives informed consent, the payer does not interfere with the lawyer's judgment or relationship with the client, and confidentiality is protected.
- **C.** Yes, but the father becomes a co-client entitled to strategy updates because he is paying.
- **D.** No, unless the client and the father both direct the defense strategy.

## 18. One Settlement for Three Clients

> **From:** Olivia Chen, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Global settlement for the apartment-fire cases
>
> We represent three tenants injured in the same apartment fire. The defendant has offered one global settlement. I told each client the total amount, the claims included, and the exact amount each tenant would receive. Each client separately agreed by telephone.
>
> The offer expires tonight. May I accept it based on their oral approvals?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Separate oral approvals are enough when every client receives the same information.
- **B.** Yes. A writing is required only if the clients receive unequal amounts.
- **C.** No. Aggregate settlements are always prohibited when one lawyer represents multiple claimants.
- **D.** No. Each client must give informed consent in a writing signed by that client after disclosure of all claims and each person's participation in the settlement.

## 19. Changing Sides on the Same Contract

> **From:** Samuel Price, Conflicts Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Former client conflict - HarborTech agreement
>
> Two years ago, you represented HarborTech while negotiating and drafting its long-term supply agreement with Meridian Parts. HarborTech is no longer a client.
>
> Meridian now wants us to sue HarborTech to rescind that same agreement based on facts connected to its negotiation. HarborTech's interests would be materially adverse, and HarborTech refuses to consent.
>
> May you represent Meridian?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. The new representation is materially adverse to a former client in the same matter, and the former client has not given informed consent confirmed in writing.
- **B.** Yes. Duties to HarborTech ended when the attorney-client relationship ended.
- **C.** Yes. The representation is permitted because the lawyer would not reveal privileged communications.
- **D.** No, but only if HarborTech can prove that the lawyer remembers a particular confidential communication.

## 20. The Prospective Client Who Was Not Retained

> **From:** Aisha Raman, Intake Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Screen after declined consultation
>
> I had a short consultation with a potential plaintiff and limited the discussion to information reasonably necessary to decide whether we could take the case. We declined. The information I received could nevertheless be significantly harmful to that person in the same dispute.
>
> The defendant later asked another team at our firm to handle the case. The firm immediately screened me from the matter, assigned me no part of its fee, and promptly sent written notice of the screen to the prospective client.
>
> Neither side has consented. May the other team proceed?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Information from any prospective client permanently disqualifies the entire firm.
- **B.** Yes, as long as the screened lawyer promises not to remember the information.
- **C.** Yes. Because the lawyer reasonably limited the initial exposure, a timely screen, no fee participation, and prompt written notice can prevent imputation to the firm.
- **D.** No. Screening is effective only if the prospective client first signs a waiver.

## 21. Splitting a Fee with the Referring Lawyer

> **From:** Ethan Wallace, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Referral fee arrangement
>
> A solo lawyer referred a products-liability case to us. The client signed a writing approving a 20/80 division of the fee, including the share each firm would receive. The total contingent fee is reasonable.
>
> The referring lawyer will perform no work and refuses to assume joint responsibility for the case. May we still divide the fee as agreed?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Written client approval makes any reasonable division permissible.
- **B.** No. Lawyers in different firms may divide a fee only in proportion to their services or when each assumes joint responsibility, with the required client agreement and a reasonable total fee.
- **C.** Yes. Referral fees are exempt from the rules governing division of fees.
- **D.** No. Lawyers in different firms may never divide a fee.

## 22. The Disputed Medical Lien

> **From:** Carmen Diaz, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Distribution of settlement funds
>
> We received a $60,000 settlement for a client and placed it in trust. A medical provider asserts a legally plausible $10,000 lien against the recovery. The client disputes the lien and demands all $60,000 immediately.
>
> The other $50,000 is not disputed. What should we do while the lien dispute is resolved?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Give the entire amount to the client because the client owns the settlement proceeds.
- **B.** Keep all $60,000 in trust until every issue is resolved.
- **C.** Pay the medical provider immediately because any asserted lien controls the client's instructions.
- **D.** Promptly distribute the undisputed $50,000 and keep the disputed $10,000 separate until the competing claims are resolved.

## 23. A Visit to the Accident Victim

> **From:** Jordan Wells, Managing Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Hospital outreach plan
>
> One of our lawyers saw a news report identifying a stranger who was injured in a train collision. Hoping to obtain a contingent-fee case, the lawyer went to the stranger's hospital room and offered to represent her. The victim had no prior family, personal, business, or professional relationship with the lawyer.
>
> Was the in-person contact proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Live person-to-person solicitation motivated significantly by pecuniary gain is prohibited when no recognized relationship or other exception applies.
- **B.** Yes. Truthful in-person solicitation is always permitted.
- **C.** Yes. The contact was permitted because the lawyer did not use coercion.
- **D.** No, but only because the victim was in a hospital.

## 24. Contacting the Company's Safety Director

> **From:** Blake Morgan, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Interview request to represented company employee
>
> We represent a plaintiff suing a manufacturer over a plant explosion. We know the manufacturer is represented in the matter.
>
> The manufacturer's current safety director supervised the response to the explosion, regularly consults with company counsel about the lawsuit, and has authority to bind the company regarding safety issues. Without seeking company counsel's consent, one of our lawyers emailed the director directly to request an interview about the explosion.
>
> Was that contact proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The no-contact rule applies only to individual parties, not organizational employees.
- **B.** Yes. An interview request is not a communication about the subject of the representation.
- **C.** No. The lawyer may not communicate about the matter with such a represented organization's constituent without counsel's consent or other legal authorization.
- **D.** No, unless the safety director first agrees to be represented personally by company counsel.

## 25. Responding to a Formal Grievance

> **From:** Nina Patel, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Former client's disciplinary complaint
>
> A former client filed a disciplinary grievance accusing me of abandoning his case and lying about work performed. Confidential information in our file disproves those allegations.
>
> May I disclose portions of the file in my response to disciplinary authorities?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Confidentiality remains absolute after the representation ends.
- **B.** Yes, but only to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes disclosure is necessary to respond to allegations concerning the representation.
- **C.** Yes. Filing a grievance waives confidentiality as to the entire client file.
- **D.** No, unless a court first orders production of the file.

## 26. False Testimony Discovered During Trial

> **From:** Victor Huang, Trial Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Client admitted his testimony was false
>
> During a civil trial, our client testified to a material fact. He has now privately admitted that the testimony was false, and I know the admission is true. I urged him to correct the testimony, but he refused.
>
> The proceeding is still pending. What must I do?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Remain silent because confidentiality always overrides candor to the tribunal.
- **B.** Withdraw without telling the court anything, even if the false evidence remains uncorrected.
- **C.** Wait to see whether opposing counsel discovers the falsehood.
- **D.** Take reasonable remedial measures, including disclosure to the tribunal if necessary, even if doing so requires revealing otherwise protected information.

## 27. The Laptop Scheduled for Destruction

> **From:** Claire Bennett, Client  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Old laptop from the contract dispute
>
> The laptop used by our former sales director contains emails relevant to the lawsuit you are handling for us. We have a legal duty to preserve them. The messages will hurt our position, and no one has specifically requested the laptop yet.
>
> Can your litigation team arrange to factory-reset it under our routine equipment-retirement policy?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A lawyer may not unlawfully destroy or conceal material with potential evidentiary value or assist a client in doing so.
- **B.** Yes. Evidence may be destroyed until the opposing party serves a specific discovery request.
- **C.** Yes. Following an ordinary retention policy eliminates any preservation obligation.
- **D.** No, but only because the laptop belonged to a former employee.

## 28. The Retaliatory Counterclaim

> **From:** Robert King, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Client insists on counterclaim
>
> Our client wants to file a counterclaim solely to increase the opponent's costs and force a cheap settlement. After investigating the facts and law, we have found no nonfrivolous basis for the claim and no good-faith argument to change existing law.
>
> The client insists that we file it anyway. What should we do?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** File it because the client controls every litigation decision.
- **B.** File it if the pleading labels the claim as legally uncertain.
- **C.** Refuse to file the frivolous claim and, if the client persists in demanding prohibited conduct, take appropriate steps concerning withdrawal.
- **D.** File it because settlement pressure is a legitimate purpose regardless of the claim's merits.

## 29. The Investigator's Pretext Call

> **From:** Danielle Ross, Associate  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Partner directed investigator to call plaintiff
>
> A partner instructed our private investigator to telephone a plaintiff whom we know is represented in the matter. The investigator was told to pose as a neutral industry researcher, avoid mentioning our firm, and obtain admissions about the lawsuit. The investigator followed those instructions.
>
> Is the partner professionally responsible for the investigator's conduct?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. The professional-conduct rules apply only to lawyers.
- **B.** Yes. A lawyer is responsible for prohibited conduct by a nonlawyer when the lawyer orders that conduct.
- **C.** No. Independent investigators are solely responsible for their own methods.
- **D.** Yes, but only if the partner later introduces the admissions in court.

## 30. The Partner's Order to Omit Authority

> **From:** Lucas Grant, Junior Associate  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Adverse case omitted from brief
>
> The controlling court recently issued a decision directly adverse to our position. Opposing counsel has not cited it. The supervising partner ordered me to omit it from our brief because "the other side can do its own research."
>
> I know the duty to disclose this controlling authority is clear rather than reasonably arguable. May I follow the partner's instruction?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A subordinate lawyer is never responsible for a supervisor's strategic decisions.
- **B.** Yes. Adverse authority need not be disclosed unless opposing counsel requests it.
- **C.** No, but only the supervising partner could be disciplined.
- **D.** No. A subordinate lawyer remains bound by the rules and cannot rely on a supervisor's direction when the ethical duty has only one reasonable answer.

## 31. Temporary Work with Local Counsel

> **From:** Harper Cole, Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Short-term representation across state lines
>
> I am admitted and in good standing in State A but not State B. A client needs temporary transactional work in State B. I will handle the matter in association with a State B lawyer who will actively participate throughout. I will not open an office in State B or claim to be admitted there.
>
> Does the model rule permit this temporary practice?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Temporary services may be provided in association with a lawyer admitted in the jurisdiction who actively participates in the matter.
- **B.** No. A lawyer may never perform any legal work while physically present in a jurisdiction where the lawyer is not admitted.
- **C.** Yes, but only if the State B lawyer performs all substantive work.
- **D.** No, unless the matter is already pending before a tribunal.

## 32. The Former Agency Lawyer

> **From:** Imani Foster, Conflicts Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Screening former government counsel
>
> A new partner formerly worked for a federal agency and participated personally and substantially in a specific enforcement investigation of Orion Systems. The partner is individually barred from representing Orion in that same matter, and the agency will not consent.
>
> Our firm proposes to represent Orion while timely screening the partner from all participation, giving the partner no part of the fee, and promptly sending written notice to the agency.
>
> May the firm accept the matter?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A former government lawyer's personal conflict can never be screened.
- **B.** Yes, if the former government lawyer informally promises not to discuss the case.
- **C.** Yes. The firm may proceed if it satisfies the timely-screening, fee-apportionment, and written-notice requirements.
- **D.** No. The entire firm is automatically treated as having participated in the government investigation.

## 33. The Bequest to the Drafting Lawyer

> **From:** Evelyn Shaw, Trusts and Estates Partner  
> **To:** You  
> **Subject:** Client wants to leave me $75,000
>
> A longtime client has asked me to prepare a new will leaving me $75,000. The client is competent, fully informed, and acting voluntarily. We are not related and do not have a close familial relationship.
>
> May I draft the will containing that gift?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A competent client's voluntary intent controls.
- **B.** No. A lawyer may not prepare an instrument giving the lawyer a substantial gift unless the lawyer is related to the client within the rule's exception.
- **C.** Yes, if another lawyer witnesses the will.
- **D.** No, because lawyers may never receive any gift from a client.

## 34. The Emergency Scheduling Call

> **From:** Hon. Teresa Alvarez  
> **To:** You, Judicial Ethics Counsel  
> **Subject:** Emergency ex parte scheduling call
>
> Plaintiff's counsel called me without defense counsel because an emergency hearing needed to be scheduled immediately. We discussed only timing and courtroom availability, not the merits. I reasonably believed no party would gain a procedural, substantive, or tactical advantage.
>
> I promptly notified defense counsel of the substance of the call and gave both sides an opportunity to respond. Was it permissible to take the call?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A judge may never receive an ex parte communication about a pending matter.
- **B.** No. Emergency communications are permitted only if every party joins the initial call.
- **C.** Yes, because scheduling communications never require notice to the absent party.
- **D.** Yes. A necessary ex parte communication for scheduling or emergency purposes may be permitted when it is nonsubstantive, creates no advantage, and is promptly disclosed with an opportunity to respond.

## 35. The Judge's Spouse Owns Stock

> **From:** Hon. Martin Ellis  
> **To:** You, Judicial Ethics Counsel  
> **Subject:** Spouse's ownership in corporate party
>
> A corporate defendant has appeared in a case assigned to me. I just learned that my spouse directly owns a significant amount of stock in that corporation. The interest is more than de minimis. I believe I can remain completely impartial, and no party has filed a motion.
>
> Am I required to disqualify myself?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A judge must disqualify when the judge knows that the judge or spouse has an economic interest in a party, regardless of the judge's subjective confidence in being impartial.
- **B.** No. Disqualification is unnecessary unless a party files a timely motion.
- **C.** No. A spouse's financial interests are not attributed to the judge.
- **D.** Yes, but only if the corporation's stock price is likely to change materially because of this particular case.

# Answer Key and Tested Rules

| Question | Answer | Primary authority | Core reason | Explanation |
|---:|:---:|---|---|---|
| 16 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.8(a) | The independent-counsel advice must be in writing, along with fair written terms and the client's signed informed consent. | Rule 1.8(a) permits a business transaction with a client only if the terms are fair and fully disclosed in writing, the client is advised in writing of the desirability of independent counsel, and the client gives informed consent in a signed writing. The oral advice to seek counsel leaves one safeguard unmet, so the purchase cannot proceed until it is given in writing; client sophistication and fair terms do not excuse the gap. |
| 17 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.8(f) | Third-party payment is allowed with informed client consent, professional independence, and protected confidentiality. | Rule 1.8(f) allows the firm to accept the father's payment because Leo gives informed consent, the father will not interfere with the lawyer's judgment or the relationship, and confidentiality will be protected. Paying the bill does not make the father a client or entitle him to strategy updates, and the rule does not require that payment come only from the person represented. |
| 18 | D | ABA Model Rule 1.8(g) | Every client in an aggregate settlement must give informed consent in a signed writing after the required disclosure. | Rule 1.8(g) requires each of the three tenants to give informed consent in a writing signed by the client, after disclosure of all claims and each person's share, before the lawyer accepts an aggregate settlement. Telephone approvals are not signed writings, the requirement applies whether or not the shares are equal, and an expiring offer does not relax it. |
| 19 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.9(a) | A lawyer may not change sides in the same or a substantially related matter without the former client's informed written consent. | Rescinding the very supply agreement you negotiated for HarborTech is the same matter, and Meridian's interests are materially adverse, so Rule 1.9(a) bars the representation without HarborTech's informed consent confirmed in writing. Duties to a former client survive the end of the relationship, and the bar does not depend on proving you remember a specific confidence. |
| 20 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.18(d)(2) | Limited intake exposure plus a timely screen, no fee share, and prompt written notice can prevent firm-wide disqualification. | Rule 1.18(d)(2) lets the firm proceed because the consultation was limited to what was reasonably necessary to decide whether to take the case, the lawyer was timely screened, receives no part of the fee, and the prospective client got prompt written notice. Those safeguards substitute for consent, so neither a blanket firm-wide bar nor a signed waiver is required. |
| 21 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.5(e) | A cross-firm fee division requires proportional services or joint responsibility, client agreement confirmed in writing, and a reasonable total fee. | Rule 1.5(e) permits lawyers in different firms to divide a fee only in proportion to the services each performs or where each assumes joint responsibility, together with client agreement confirmed in writing and a reasonable total fee. The referring lawyer will do no work and refuses joint responsibility, so the client's signed approval cannot by itself make the 20/80 split permissible. |
| 22 | D | ABA Model Rule 1.15(e) | The disputed amount stays separate, but undisputed funds must be promptly distributed. | Rule 1.15(e) requires the disputed $10,000 to stay in trust until the client and the medical provider resolve their competing claims, while the undisputed $50,000 must be delivered promptly. Paying everything to the client ignores the plausible lien, paying the provider ignores the client's dispute, and holding all $60,000 wrongly withholds funds the client is clearly owed. |
| 23 | A | ABA Model Rule 7.3(b) | Live solicitation for pecuniary gain is barred unless a listed relationship or other exception applies. | Rule 7.3(b) prohibits live person-to-person solicitation when a significant motive is pecuniary gain and the person contacted is not a lawyer, a family member, a close friend or a prior client. Walking into a stranger's hospital room with a contingent-fee pitch is the paradigm violation; the absence of coercion and the hospital setting are beside the point. |
| 24 | C | ABA Model Rule 4.2 | The safety director falls within the represented organization's protected constituents for communications about the matter. | The safety director supervised the response, regularly consults company counsel about the suit, and can bind the company on safety issues, so Rule 4.2 treats her as a constituent of the represented organization whom the lawyer may not contact about the matter without counsel's consent. An interview request about the explosion is a communication about the subject of the representation. |
| 25 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.6(b)(5) | A lawyer may disclose only what is reasonably necessary to respond to allegations concerning the representation. | Rule 1.6(b)(5) permits disclosure to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary to respond to allegations in any proceeding concerning the representation, so the partner may use file material that disproves the abandonment and lying claims. Confidentiality is not absolute after the representation ends, but the grievance does not waive the whole file; only what is needed to respond may be revealed. |
| 26 | D | ABA Model Rule 3.3(a)(3), (c) | Known material false evidence requires reasonable remediation, including disclosure if necessary, despite confidentiality. | Rule 3.3(a)(3) requires a lawyer who comes to know that a client's material testimony was false to take reasonable remedial measures, including disclosure to the tribunal if necessary, and Rule 3.3(c) makes that duty override confidentiality while the proceeding continues. Silence, silent withdrawal, or waiting for the other side to notice all leave the false evidence uncorrected. |
| 27 | A | ABA Model Rule 3.4(a) | A lawyer may not unlawfully destroy or conceal potentially relevant evidence or assist a client in doing so. | Rule 3.4(a) prohibits unlawfully destroying or concealing material with potential evidentiary value and assisting a client in doing so; the company already has a duty to preserve the emails, so a factory reset under a routine policy is spoliation. A specific discovery request is not the trigger, and the laptop's former user is irrelevant. |
| 28 | C | ABA Model Rule 3.1 | The lawyer must refuse a claim with no nonfrivolous factual or legal basis. | Rule 3.1 forbids asserting a claim with no nonfrivolous basis in fact or law and no good-faith argument for changing existing law, so the firm must refuse the counterclaim even though the client insists. The client controls objectives but not whether the lawyer files frivolous pleadings, and labeling the claim uncertain or citing settlement pressure does not supply the missing basis. |
| 29 | B | ABA Model Rule 5.3(c)(1) | A lawyer is responsible when the lawyer orders a nonlawyer to engage in conduct that would violate the rules if done by a lawyer. | Rule 5.3(c)(1) makes a lawyer responsible for a nonlawyer's conduct that would violate the rules if the lawyer ordered it, and the partner ordered a pretext call to a represented plaintiff that would breach Rules 4.2 and 8.4(c) if the partner had made it. The rules reach the lawyer who directs the investigator, whether or not the admissions are ever used. |
| 30 | D | ABA Model Rules 5.2 and 3.3(a)(2) | Following orders does not excuse a clear violation; protection applies only to a supervisor's reasonable resolution of an arguable duty. | Rule 3.3(a)(2) requires disclosing directly adverse controlling authority the opponent has not cited, and because that duty is clear rather than reasonably arguable, Rule 5.2(b) gives the associate no safe harbor for following the partner's order. Rule 5.2(a) makes the subordinate equally responsible, so both lawyers could be disciplined. |
| 31 | A | ABA Model Rule 5.5(c)(1) | Temporary work is permitted in association with an admitted local lawyer who actively participates. | Rule 5.5(c)(1) permits a lawyer admitted in State A to provide temporary legal services in State B in association with a State B lawyer who actively participates in the matter, without opening a State B office or claiming admission there. The local lawyer must actively participate, not do all the substantive work, and the exception does not depend on a pending tribunal matter. |
| 32 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.11(b) | A former government lawyer's personal conflict need not disqualify the firm if the specified screen, fee, and notice conditions are met. | Rule 1.11(b) allows the firm to represent Orion even though the former agency partner is personally disqualified, provided the partner is timely screened, receives no part of the fee, and the agency gets prompt written notice. The firm is not automatically tainted, and an informal promise is no substitute for the structural screen. |
| 33 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.8(c) | A lawyer generally may not draft an instrument giving the lawyer a substantial gift from an unrelated client. | Rule 1.8(c) prohibits preparing an instrument that gives the lawyer a substantial gift from a client unless the lawyer is related to the client, so you may not draft a will leaving yourself $75,000 from an unrelated client, however voluntary her wish. Another lawyer witnessing the will does not cure the problem; the client needs independent counsel to draft the gift. |
| 34 | D | ABA Model Code of Judicial Conduct Rule 2.9(A)(1) | Necessary nonsubstantive scheduling or emergency communications are permitted with no advantage and prompt notice and response rights. | Judicial Conduct Rule 2.9(A)(1) permits an ex parte communication for scheduling or emergency purposes when it does not address substantive matters, the judge reasonably believes no party will gain an advantage, and the judge promptly notifies the other parties and lets them respond. Judge Alvarez met each condition; the rule does not require every party on the initial call, but the prompt notice was essential. |
| 35 | A | ABA Model Code of Judicial Conduct Rule 2.11(A)(3) | A judge must disqualify when the judge or spouse has a qualifying economic interest in a party. | Judicial Conduct Rule 2.11(A)(3) requires disqualification when the judge knows that the judge or the judge's spouse has a more than de minimis economic interest in a party, and the spouse's holding counts regardless of the judge's confidence in his own impartiality. No party motion is needed, and whether the case will move the stock price is not the test. |
