# MPRE Associate Email Scenarios: 41 Additional Questions

## Instructions

Assume you are a law-firm associate, government lawyer, or judicial-ethics lawyer receiving each email below. Select the best response under the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct or, where indicated, the ABA Model Code of Judicial Conduct.

These are original educational hypotheticals. Their topic mix and fact-pattern difficulty were inspired by the linked Quizlet MPRE study set, but the scenarios and answer choices were newly written and do not reproduce the Quizlet cards. They are not official NCBE questions, and state rules may differ from the ABA models.

## 36. The E-Discovery Platform

> **From:** Morgan Li, Litigation Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Can we run the document production ourselves?
>
> We must review and produce several million emails. No one on our team understands the new review platform, its privilege filters, or how it handles metadata. The client wants to avoid the cost of an e-discovery specialist.
>
> I am considering having a junior associate upload the files and begin production immediately, learning the platform along the way. What do our professional obligations require?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** The proposed approach is proper because technological tasks are outside a lawyer's duty of competence.
- **B.** The team must obtain the necessary technological competence through study or qualified assistance before using the platform in a way that risks the client's matter.
- **C.** The proposed approach is proper if the client signs a waiver of competent representation.
- **D.** Only the partner needs technological competence; the lawyers performing the review do not.

## 37. Accepting the Settlement Without Calling

> **From:** Sophia Reed, Client
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Did the firm have authority to settle?
>
> I authorized the firm to negotiate my employment claim, but I never authorized anyone to accept a particular amount. Yesterday, opposing counsel offered $180,000. Your partner believed it was an excellent result and accepted it before consulting me.
>
> Was the partner permitted to make that decision?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer controls all decisions made during settlement negotiations.
- **B.** Yes. General authority to negotiate automatically includes authority to accept any reasonable offer.
- **C.** No, but only because the offer exceeded $100,000.
- **D.** No. The client decides whether to settle, and the lawyer lacked authority to accept the offer.

## 38. Drafting Only the Answer

> **From:** Amelia Grant, Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Limited-scope landlord matter
>
> A small-business owner cannot afford full representation in a straightforward eviction case. She wants us only to analyze the complaint and draft her answer; she will appear without us after that. The limitation is reasonable under the circumstances.
>
> We carefully explained what we will and will not do, and she gave informed consent. May we accept the limited engagement?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may reasonably limit the scope of a representation when the client gives informed consent.
- **B.** No. Every litigation engagement must continue through final judgment.
- **C.** Yes, but only if opposing counsel consents.
- **D.** No. A client may never appear without counsel after receiving limited legal assistance.

## 39. Explaining the Criminal-Law Boundary

> **From:** Daniel Cho, Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Advice about a proposed payment system
>
> A business client described a proposed payment system that might violate federal reporting laws. I explained the statutes, the possible criminal consequences, and the unresolved legal questions. I advised the client not to proceed unless the plan could be brought into compliance.
>
> I did not suggest concealment or help implement an unlawful plan. The client later used my legal analysis to design a criminal scheme without telling me. Did my original advice violate the rules?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may not discuss the legal consequences of conduct that might become criminal.
- **B.** Yes. A client's later misuse of advice automatically makes the lawyer a participant.
- **C.** No. A lawyer may explain legal consequences and help a client make a good-faith determination of the law's validity, scope, meaning, or application.
- **D.** No, but only because the lawyer did not charge for implementing the scheme.

## 40. The Uncommunicated Plea Offer

> **From:** Eric Nolan, Criminal Defense Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Plea offer I intend to reject
>
> The prosecutor offered our client a plea carrying six months in custody instead of the five-year sentence charged. I believe our suppression motion will win, and the client previously told me only that he hoped to avoid incarceration.
>
> I plan to reject the offer without telling him because discussing it may weaken his resolve. Is that permissible?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Defense counsel may withhold any offer the lawyer reasonably believes is unfavorable.
- **B.** No. A lawyer must promptly communicate a plea offer unless the client has previously authorized its acceptance or rejection.
- **C.** Yes. Litigation strategy is exclusively the lawyer's decision.
- **D.** No, but only if the suppression motion ultimately fails.

## 41. The Missed Renewal Filing

> **From:** Lena Brooks, Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Error in ongoing patent matter
>
> While we continue to represent a technology client, I discovered that our team missed a renewal filing. The error materially damaged the client's rights and could cause the client to reconsider both its strategy and whether to keep our firm.
>
> We may be able to mitigate some of the harm. Must we tell the client about the error now?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A material error in an ongoing representation must be disclosed when a reasonable client would consider it important to the representation or the lawyer-client relationship.
- **B.** No. The firm may wait until all possible corrective measures have failed.
- **C.** No. A lawyer never has to disclose the lawyer's own mistake.
- **D.** Yes, but only if the client first asks whether an error occurred.

## 42. The Omitted Discipline on a Bar Application

> **From:** Admissions Committee Counsel
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Applicant's incomplete disclosure
>
> A bar applicant knowingly omitted a prior professional-license suspension from his application. During the admissions investigation, the committee directly asked whether his filing was complete. He repeated the false answer and refused to correct the record.
>
> Is the applicant's conduct proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Bar applicants are not bound by professional-conduct rules until admission.
- **B.** Yes. A licensing suspension outside the legal profession is never relevant.
- **C.** No, but only if the omission would automatically disqualify the applicant.
- **D.** No. An applicant may not knowingly make a material false statement or fail to correct a known misapprehension in an admissions matter.

## 43. A Contingent Fee for an Acquittal

- **D.** No, unless the prosecutor also approves the arrangement.

## 44. Recovering Past-Due Support

> **From:** Marisol Vega, Family-Law Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Contingent fee for unpaid support judgment
>
> A former spouse already has a final court order awarding support. The obligor has ignored it for three years. The client wants us to pursue the accumulated unpaid balance for a reasonable percentage of whatever we recover.
>
> Would that contingent-fee arrangement be permissible?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Contingent fees are prohibited in every matter connected to domestic relations.
- **B.** Yes. The prohibition does not bar a reasonable contingent fee to recover post-judgment amounts already due under an existing support order.
- **C.** Yes, but only if the former spouses remarry first.
- **D.** No, because any fee based on a financial recovery is necessarily unreasonable.

## 46. The Final Disclosure Order

## 47. Misconduct Learned from the Client

> **From:** Alex Moreno, Partner

> **Subject:** Reporting a former lawyer
>
> During a confidential consultation, our current client gave us reliable information showing that her former lawyer stole money from her trust account. The conduct raises a substantial question about that lawyer's honesty. Our client refuses to authorize disclosure, and no confidentiality exception applies.

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The reporting rule always overrides client confidentiality.
- **B.** Yes. Theft from a trust account can never be protected information.
- **C.** No. The reporting rule does not require disclosure of information otherwise protected by the confidentiality rule.
- **D.** No, because trust-account theft does not concern a lawyer's honesty or fitness.

## 48. Buying the Property Next Door

> **From:** Priya Nair, General Counsel
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Lawyer used our confidential expansion plans
>
> Our outside lawyer learned during the representation that we planned to acquire a specific warehouse. Before we could make an offer, the lawyer secretly purchased the neighboring parcel because our project would increase its value.
>
> The lawyer did not reveal our information to anyone, but used it for personal gain without our consent. Was that proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The rule prohibits disclosure, not personal use.
- **B.** No. A lawyer may not use information relating to a representation to the client's disadvantage without informed consent or another rule-based authorization.
- **C.** Yes. The neighboring parcel was not itself the subject of the representation.
- **D.** No, but only if the client's plan was protected by the evidentiary privilege.

## 49. Suing an Existing Client in an Unrelated Matter

> **From:** James Porter, Conflicts Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Direct adversity to Alpha Foods
>
> Our regulatory group currently represents Alpha Foods. A potential new client wants our litigation group to sue Alpha in a completely unrelated lease dispute. The two teams would share no confidential information, but Alpha refuses to consent.
>
> May the firm accept the lawsuit?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Suing a current client creates direct adversity even when the matters are unrelated, and the required informed consent has not been obtained.
- **B.** Yes. Unrelated matters can never create a current-client conflict.
- **C.** Yes. Ethical walls automatically cure direct adversity between current clients.
- **D.** No, but only if the lease dispute is substantially related to the regulatory work.

## 51. The Sophisticated Client's Advance Waiver

> **From:** Teresa Wu, Conflicts Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Scope of advance conflict waiver
>
> A sophisticated corporate client, advised by independent counsel, signed an advance waiver. The document specifically identified the types of unrelated matters in which we might oppose the company, described the likely classes of adverse clients, and explained the material risks.
>
> A new, consentable, unrelated matter falls squarely within that description. Is the advance waiver necessarily ineffective merely because it was signed before the conflict arose?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Consent can be informed only after a conflict has matured.
- **B.** Yes. Advance waivers are valid only for individual clients.
- **C.** No. A sufficiently specific waiver by a sophisticated client may constitute informed consent to a future consentable conflict.
- **D.** No. Any general statement authorizing all future conflicts is automatically effective.

## 52. A Lawyer's Family Relationship

> **From:** Celeste Martin, Managing Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Is this personal conflict firm-wide?
>
> One of our lawyers cannot represent a plaintiff because the defendant's CEO is the lawyer's sibling, creating a significant personal-interest conflict. Another lawyer at our firm has no relationship with the sibling, and there is no significant risk that the first lawyer's personal interest will materially limit the second lawyer's representation.
>
> Is the second lawyer automatically disqualified?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Every conflict of one lawyer is imputed to the entire firm.
- **B.** No. A purely personal-interest conflict is not imputed when it creates no significant risk of materially limiting the other lawyers.
- **C.** Yes. Family relationships always create nonwaivable firm-wide conflicts.
- **D.** No, but only if the opposing party consents.

## 53. The Lateral Who Learned Nothing

> **From:** Omar Fields, Conflicts Counsel

- **A.** Yes. Employment at the former firm creates an irrebuttable presumption of knowledge.
- **B.** Yes. Every client of a lawyer's former firm becomes that lawyer's former client.
- **C.** No, but only if the plaintiff gives informed written consent.
- **D.** No. The moving lawyer is not barred under the former-firm rule without protected information material to the matter.

## 55. Groceries for a Pro Bono Client

> **From:** Hannah Bell, Pro Bono Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Emergency grocery assistance
>
> I represent an indigent tenant without charging a fee. After the representation began, I learned that she could not buy food for her children. I gave her a modest grocery card.
>
> I never promised assistance before retention, did not use it to induce her to remain a client, will not seek repayment, and do not advertise such gifts. Was the gift permissible?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A lawyer may provide only court costs, never basic living assistance.
- **B.** No. Any financial help creates an impermissible proprietary interest in the case.
- **C.** Yes. The model rule permits a modest basic-needs gift to an indigent pro bono client when its safeguards are satisfied.
- **D.** Yes, but only if the lawyer deducts the amount from a future recovery.

## 61. Selling an Entire Practice Area

> **From:** Judith Evans, Retiring Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Sale of immigration practice
>
> I plan to sell my entire immigration-law practice to another competent firm and stop accepting immigration matters. Each client will receive written notice of the sale, the proposed purchaser, the right to choose other counsel or retrieve the file, and the 90-day consent rule. Client fees will not increase because of the sale.
>
> Is such a sale permissible under the model rule?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may sell an entire practice or an entire practice area when the rule's cessation, notice, client-choice, and fee protections are satisfied.
- **B.** No. The goodwill of a law practice may never be sold.
- **C.** Yes, but the clients lose their right to select other counsel.
- **D.** No, unless the seller also leaves every other area of legal practice.

## 62. Moral Considerations in Legal Advice

> **From:** Theo Grant, Client
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Advice about closing our only rural plant
>
> Closing our plant would be lawful and profitable, but it would eliminate most jobs in a small town. I asked our lawyer for legal advice. She also discussed the likely moral, economic, and social consequences of the closure.
>
> Was it proper for her to address considerations beyond the letter of the law?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Legal advice must exclude all moral and social considerations.
- **B.** No. Discussing economic consequences converts the lawyer into a business partner.
- **C.** Yes, but only if the lawyer is also licensed as a financial adviser.
- **D.** Yes. In giving candid advice, a lawyer may refer to moral, economic, social, and political factors relevant to the client's situation.

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A client may choose any lawful litigation tactic.

> **Subject:** Opposing counsel accidentally copied us
>
> Opposing counsel mistakenly emailed us a document that was obviously intended for the opposing client and appears privileged. We recognized the mistake immediately.

## 74. Paying the Marketing Company a Percentage

> **From:** Firm Marketing Director
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Performance compensation proposal
>
> An online marketing company proposes receiving 5% of every legal fee collected from clients generated by its advertising campaign. The company is not owned by lawyers and will perform no legal work.
>
> May the firm accept that compensation structure?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Nonlawyers may receive any percentage if they perform marketing work.
- **B.** Yes. The arrangement is permissible if the total fee charged to each client is reasonable.
- **C.** No, but only if the marketing company influences litigation strategy.
- **D.** No. Paying a nonlawyer a percentage of legal fees is prohibited fee sharing unless a specific exception applies.

## 75. The Associate's Noncompetition Clause

> **From:** William Scott, Managing Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Two-year practice restriction
>
> Our proposed employment agreement says that, after leaving the firm, an associate may not practice family law anywhere in the county for two years. The restriction is unrelated to retirement benefits.
>
> Is the clause proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Law firms may use the same noncompetition clauses as other businesses.
- **B.** Yes. The restriction is proper because it lasts only two years.
- **C.** No. A lawyer may not participate in an employment agreement restricting the right to practice after termination, except for an agreement concerning retirement benefits.
- **D.** No, but only if the associate already has clients in the county.

## 76. The Judicial Candidate's Eviction Promise

> **From:** Judicial Campaign Committee
> **To:** You, Campaign Ethics Counsel
> **Subject:** Proposed statement about rent-withholding cases
>
> A judicial candidate wants to say: "If elected, I promise that I will always order eviction whenever a tenant withholds rent, regardless of the tenant's defense."
>
> May the candidate make that promise?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A judicial candidate may not make pledges, promises, or commitments about likely cases that are inconsistent with impartial performance of judicial duties.
- **B.** Yes. Campaign statements can never violate judicial-conduct rules.
- **C.** Yes. The promise is permissible because landlord-tenant law is a public issue.
- **D.** No, but only if an eviction case is already pending before that candidate.
