# MPRE Associate Email Scenarios: 41 Additional Questions

## Instructions

Assume you are a law-firm associate, government lawyer, or judicial-ethics lawyer receiving each email below. Select the best response under the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct or, where indicated, the ABA Model Code of Judicial Conduct.

These are original educational hypotheticals. Their topic mix and fact-pattern difficulty were inspired by the linked Quizlet MPRE study set, but the scenarios and answer choices were newly written and do not reproduce the Quizlet cards. They are not official NCBE questions, and state rules may differ from the ABA models.

## 36. The E-Discovery Platform

> **From:** Morgan Li, Litigation Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Can we run the document production ourselves?
>
> We must review and produce several million emails. No one on our team understands the new review platform, its privilege filters, or how it handles metadata. The client wants to avoid the cost of an e-discovery specialist.
>
> I am considering having a junior associate upload the files and begin production immediately, learning the platform along the way. What do our professional obligations require?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** The proposed approach is proper because technological tasks are outside a lawyer's duty of competence.
- **B.** The team must obtain the necessary technological competence through study or qualified assistance before using the platform in a way that risks the client's matter.
- **C.** The proposed approach is proper if the client signs a waiver of competent representation.
- **D.** Only the partner needs technological competence; the lawyers performing the review do not.

## 37. Accepting the Settlement Without Calling

> **From:** Sophia Reed, Client
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Did the firm have authority to settle?
>
> I authorized the firm to negotiate my employment claim, but I never authorized anyone to accept a particular amount. Yesterday, opposing counsel offered $180,000. Your partner believed it was an excellent result and accepted it before consulting me.
>
> Was the partner permitted to make that decision?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer controls all decisions made during settlement negotiations.
- **B.** Yes. General authority to negotiate automatically includes authority to accept any reasonable offer.
- **C.** No, but only because the offer exceeded $100,000.
- **D.** No. The client decides whether to settle, and the lawyer lacked authority to accept the offer.

## 38. Drafting Only the Answer

> **From:** Amelia Grant, Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Limited-scope landlord matter
>
> A small-business owner cannot afford full representation in a straightforward eviction case. She wants us only to analyze the complaint and draft her answer; she will appear without us after that. The limitation is reasonable under the circumstances.
>
> We carefully explained what we will and will not do, and she gave informed consent. May we accept the limited engagement?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may reasonably limit the scope of a representation when the client gives informed consent.
- **B.** No. Every litigation engagement must continue through final judgment.
- **C.** Yes, but only if opposing counsel consents.
- **D.** No. A client may never appear without counsel after receiving limited legal assistance.

## 39. Explaining the Criminal-Law Boundary

> **From:** Daniel Cho, Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Advice about a proposed payment system
>
> A business client described a proposed payment system that might violate federal reporting laws. I explained the statutes, the possible criminal consequences, and the unresolved legal questions. I advised the client not to proceed unless the plan could be brought into compliance.
>
> I did not suggest concealment or help implement an unlawful plan. The client later used my legal analysis to design a criminal scheme without telling me. Did my original advice violate the rules?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may not discuss the legal consequences of conduct that might become criminal.
- **B.** Yes. A client's later misuse of advice automatically makes the lawyer a participant.
- **C.** No. A lawyer may explain legal consequences and help a client make a good-faith determination of the law's validity, scope, meaning, or application.
- **D.** No, but only because the lawyer did not charge for implementing the scheme.

## 40. The Uncommunicated Plea Offer

> **From:** Eric Nolan, Criminal Defense Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Plea offer I intend to reject
>
> The prosecutor offered our client a plea carrying six months in custody instead of the five-year sentence charged. I believe our suppression motion will win, and the client previously told me only that he hoped to avoid incarceration.
>
> I plan to reject the offer without telling him because discussing it may weaken his resolve. Is that permissible?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Defense counsel may withhold any offer the lawyer reasonably believes is unfavorable.
- **B.** No. A lawyer must promptly communicate a plea offer unless the client has previously authorized its acceptance or rejection.
- **C.** Yes. Litigation strategy is exclusively the lawyer's decision.
- **D.** No, but only if the suppression motion ultimately fails.

## 41. The Missed Renewal Filing

> **From:** Lena Brooks, Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Error in ongoing patent matter
>
> While we continue to represent a technology client, I discovered that our team missed a renewal filing. The error materially damaged the client's rights and could cause the client to reconsider both its strategy and whether to keep our firm.
>
> We may be able to mitigate some of the harm. Must we tell the client about the error now?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A material error in an ongoing representation must be disclosed when a reasonable client would consider it important to the representation or the lawyer-client relationship.
- **B.** No. The firm may wait until all possible corrective measures have failed.
- **C.** No. A lawyer never has to disclose the lawyer's own mistake.
- **D.** Yes, but only if the client first asks whether an error occurred.

## 42. The Omitted Discipline on a Bar Application

> **From:** Admissions Committee Counsel
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Applicant's incomplete disclosure
>
> A bar applicant knowingly omitted a prior professional-license suspension from his application. During the admissions investigation, the committee directly asked whether his filing was complete. He repeated the false answer and refused to correct the record.
>
> Is the applicant's conduct proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Bar applicants are not bound by professional-conduct rules until admission.
- **B.** Yes. A licensing suspension outside the legal profession is never relevant.
- **C.** No, but only if the omission would automatically disqualify the applicant.
- **D.** No. An applicant may not knowingly make a material false statement or fail to correct a known misapprehension in an admissions matter.

## 43. A Contingent Fee for an Acquittal

- **D.** No, unless the prosecutor also approves the arrangement.

## 44. Recovering Past-Due Support

> **From:** Marisol Vega, Family-Law Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Contingent fee for unpaid support judgment
>
> A former spouse already has a final court order awarding support. The obligor has ignored it for three years. The client wants us to pursue the accumulated unpaid balance for a reasonable percentage of whatever we recover.
>
> Would that contingent-fee arrangement be permissible?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Contingent fees are prohibited in every matter connected to domestic relations.
- **B.** Yes. The prohibition does not bar a reasonable contingent fee to recover post-judgment amounts already due under an existing support order.
- **C.** Yes, but only if the former spouses remarry first.
- **D.** No, because any fee based on a financial recovery is necessarily unreasonable.

## 46. The Final Disclosure Order

## 47. Misconduct Learned from the Client

> **From:** Alex Moreno, Partner

> **Subject:** Reporting a former lawyer
>
> During a confidential consultation, our current client gave us reliable information showing that her former lawyer stole money from her trust account. The conduct raises a substantial question about that lawyer's honesty. Our client refuses to authorize disclosure, and no confidentiality exception applies.

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The reporting rule always overrides client confidentiality.
- **B.** Yes. Theft from a trust account can never be protected information.
- **C.** No. The reporting rule does not require disclosure of information otherwise protected by the confidentiality rule.
- **D.** No, because trust-account theft does not concern a lawyer's honesty or fitness.

## 48. Buying the Property Next Door

> **From:** Priya Nair, General Counsel
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Lawyer used our confidential expansion plans
>
> Our outside lawyer learned during the representation that we planned to acquire a specific warehouse. Before we could make an offer, the lawyer secretly purchased the neighboring parcel because our project would increase its value.
>
> The lawyer did not reveal our information to anyone, but used it for personal gain without our consent. Was that proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. The rule prohibits disclosure, not personal use.
- **B.** No. A lawyer may not use information relating to a representation to the client's disadvantage without informed consent or another rule-based authorization.
- **C.** Yes. The neighboring parcel was not itself the subject of the representation.
- **D.** No, but only if the client's plan was protected by the evidentiary privilege.

## 49. Suing an Existing Client in an Unrelated Matter

> **From:** James Porter, Conflicts Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Direct adversity to Alpha Foods
>
> Our regulatory group currently represents Alpha Foods. A potential new client wants our litigation group to sue Alpha in a completely unrelated lease dispute. The two teams would share no confidential information, but Alpha refuses to consent.
>
> May the firm accept the lawsuit?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Suing a current client creates direct adversity even when the matters are unrelated, and the required informed consent has not been obtained.
- **B.** Yes. Unrelated matters can never create a current-client conflict.
- **C.** Yes. Ethical walls automatically cure direct adversity between current clients.
- **D.** No, but only if the lease dispute is substantially related to the regulatory work.

## 51. The Sophisticated Client's Advance Waiver

> **From:** Teresa Wu, Conflicts Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Scope of advance conflict waiver
>
> A sophisticated corporate client, advised by independent counsel, signed an advance waiver. The document specifically identified the types of unrelated matters in which we might oppose the company, described the likely classes of adverse clients, and explained the material risks.
>
> A new, consentable, unrelated matter falls squarely within that description. Is the advance waiver necessarily ineffective merely because it was signed before the conflict arose?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Consent can be informed only after a conflict has matured.
- **B.** Yes. Advance waivers are valid only for individual clients.
- **C.** No. A sufficiently specific waiver by a sophisticated client may constitute informed consent to a future consentable conflict.
- **D.** No. Any general statement authorizing all future conflicts is automatically effective.

## 52. A Lawyer's Family Relationship

> **From:** Celeste Martin, Managing Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Is this personal conflict firm-wide?
>
> One of our lawyers cannot represent a plaintiff because the defendant's CEO is the lawyer's sibling, creating a significant personal-interest conflict. Another lawyer at our firm has no relationship with the sibling, and there is no significant risk that the first lawyer's personal interest will materially limit the second lawyer's representation.
>
> Is the second lawyer automatically disqualified?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Every conflict of one lawyer is imputed to the entire firm.
- **B.** No. A purely personal-interest conflict is not imputed when it creates no significant risk of materially limiting the other lawyers.
- **C.** Yes. Family relationships always create nonwaivable firm-wide conflicts.
- **D.** No, but only if the opposing party consents.

## 53. The Lateral Who Learned Nothing

> **From:** Omar Fields, Conflicts Counsel

- **A.** Yes. Employment at the former firm creates an irrebuttable presumption of knowledge.
- **B.** Yes. Every client of a lawyer's former firm becomes that lawyer's former client.
- **C.** No, but only if the plaintiff gives informed written consent.
- **D.** No. The moving lawyer is not barred under the former-firm rule without protected information material to the matter.

## 55. Groceries for a Pro Bono Client

> **From:** Hannah Bell, Pro Bono Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Emergency grocery assistance
>
> I represent an indigent tenant without charging a fee. After the representation began, I learned that she could not buy food for her children. I gave her a modest grocery card.
>
> I never promised assistance before retention, did not use it to induce her to remain a client, will not seek repayment, and do not advertise such gifts. Was the gift permissible?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A lawyer may provide only court costs, never basic living assistance.
- **B.** No. Any financial help creates an impermissible proprietary interest in the case.
- **C.** Yes. The model rule permits a modest basic-needs gift to an indigent pro bono client when its safeguards are satisfied.
- **D.** Yes, but only if the lawyer deducts the amount from a future recovery.

## 61. Selling an Entire Practice Area

> **From:** Judith Evans, Retiring Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Sale of immigration practice
>
> I plan to sell my entire immigration-law practice to another competent firm and stop accepting immigration matters. Each client will receive written notice of the sale, the proposed purchaser, the right to choose other counsel or retrieve the file, and the 90-day consent rule. Client fees will not increase because of the sale.
>
> Is such a sale permissible under the model rule?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A lawyer may sell an entire practice or an entire practice area when the rule's cessation, notice, client-choice, and fee protections are satisfied.
- **B.** No. The goodwill of a law practice may never be sold.
- **C.** Yes, but the clients lose their right to select other counsel.
- **D.** No, unless the seller also leaves every other area of legal practice.

## 62. Moral Considerations in Legal Advice

> **From:** Theo Grant, Client
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Advice about closing our only rural plant
>
> Closing our plant would be lawful and profitable, but it would eliminate most jobs in a small town. I asked our lawyer for legal advice. She also discussed the likely moral, economic, and social consequences of the closure.
>
> Was it proper for her to address considerations beyond the letter of the law?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Legal advice must exclude all moral and social considerations.
- **B.** No. Discussing economic consequences converts the lawyer into a business partner.
- **C.** Yes, but only if the lawyer is also licensed as a financial adviser.
- **D.** Yes. In giving candid advice, a lawyer may refer to moral, economic, social, and political factors relevant to the client's situation.

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A client may choose any lawful litigation tactic.

> **Subject:** Opposing counsel accidentally copied us
>
> Opposing counsel mistakenly emailed us a document that was obviously intended for the opposing client and appears privileged. We recognized the mistake immediately.

> What does the model rule require us to do?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **B.** Promptly notify the sender; questions about return, destruction, or use are determined by other law or professional judgment beyond the notification requirement.
- **C.** Read and distribute it immediately because accidental transmission waives every protection.

## 74. Paying the Marketing Company a Percentage

> **From:** Firm Marketing Director
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Performance compensation proposal
>
> An online marketing company proposes receiving 5% of every legal fee collected from clients generated by its advertising campaign. The company is not owned by lawyers and will perform no legal work.
>
> May the firm accept that compensation structure?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Nonlawyers may receive any percentage if they perform marketing work.
- **B.** Yes. The arrangement is permissible if the total fee charged to each client is reasonable.
- **C.** No, but only if the marketing company influences litigation strategy.
- **D.** No. Paying a nonlawyer a percentage of legal fees is prohibited fee sharing unless a specific exception applies.

## 75. The Associate's Noncompetition Clause

> **From:** William Scott, Managing Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Two-year practice restriction
>
> Our proposed employment agreement says that, after leaving the firm, an associate may not practice family law anywhere in the county for two years. The restriction is unrelated to retirement benefits.
>
> Is the clause proper?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. Law firms may use the same noncompetition clauses as other businesses.
- **B.** Yes. The restriction is proper because it lasts only two years.
- **C.** No. A lawyer may not participate in an employment agreement restricting the right to practice after termination, except for an agreement concerning retirement benefits.
- **D.** No, but only if the associate already has clients in the county.

## 76. The Judicial Candidate's Eviction Promise

> **From:** Judicial Campaign Committee
> **To:** You, Campaign Ethics Counsel
> **Subject:** Proposed statement about rent-withholding cases
>
> A judicial candidate wants to say: "If elected, I promise that I will always order eviction whenever a tenant withholds rent, regardless of the tenant's defense."
>
> May the candidate make that promise?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A judicial candidate may not make pledges, promises, or commitments about likely cases that are inconsistent with impartial performance of judicial duties.
- **B.** Yes. Campaign statements can never violate judicial-conduct rules.
- **C.** Yes. The promise is permissible because landlord-tenant law is a public issue.
- **D.** No, but only if an eviction case is already pending before that candidate.

| 36 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.1, Comment 8 | Competence includes keeping abreast of relevant technology or obtaining qualified assistance before using it. | Rule 1.1, Comment 8 makes keeping abreast of relevant technology part of competence, so before uploading millions of emails to a platform no one understands the team must learn it or bring in qualified assistance. Competence cannot be waived by the client and applies to every lawyer doing the review, not just the partner. |
| 37 | D | ABA Model Rule 1.2(a) | The client decides whether to settle; negotiation authority alone does not necessarily authorize acceptance. | Rule 1.2(a) reserves the decision whether to settle a civil matter to the client, and authority to negotiate is not authority to accept, so the partner could not bind Ms. Reed to the $180,000 offer however good it looked. The size of the offer is irrelevant; the problem is the missing client decision. |
| 38 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.2(c) | A representation may be reasonably limited when the client gives informed consent. | Rule 1.2(c) permits limiting the scope of a representation when the limitation is reasonable under the circumstances and the client gives informed consent, both of which are met by analyzing the complaint and drafting the answer for the self-represented owner. Neither opposing counsel's consent nor continuation through judgment is required. |
| 39 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.2(d), Comment 9 | A lawyer may explain legal consequences and the law's application but may not counsel or assist crime or fraud. | Rule 1.2(d) and Comment 9 distinguish presenting an honest analysis of the legal consequences of a proposed course of action, which is permitted, from recommending or assisting a crime or fraud, which is not; the partner explained the statutes and advised against proceeding. The client's secret later misuse of that advice does not retroactively make the lawyer a participant. |
| 40 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.4, Comment 2 | A lawyer must promptly communicate a plea offer unless the client has already authorized acceptance or rejection. | Rule 1.4, Comment 2 requires promptly informing a criminal client of a plea offer unless the client has previously told the lawyer that such an offer is acceptable or unacceptable. The client's stated hope to avoid incarceration is not advance authority to reject a six-month deal, and confidence in the suppression motion does not entitle counsel to decide for him. |
| 41 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.4; ABA Formal Opinion 481 | A material error in an ongoing representation must be disclosed when a reasonable client would consider it important. | Rule 1.4 requires keeping the client reasonably informed, and ABA Formal Opinion 481 reads that duty to require disclosure of a material error in an ongoing representation when a reasonable client would consider it important; a missed renewal filing that damaged the client's rights qualifies. The firm cannot wait for mitigation to fail or for the client to ask. |
| 42 | D | ABA Model Rule 8.1 | A bar applicant may not knowingly make a material false statement or fail to correct a known misapprehension. | Rule 8.1 prohibits a bar applicant from knowingly making a false statement of material fact or failing to correct a misapprehension known to have arisen in the admissions matter. The applicant omitted the suspension and then repeated the false answer when asked directly, so the conduct is improper whether or not the suspension would have been disqualifying. |
| 43 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.5(d)(2) | Contingent fees are prohibited for representing a defendant in a criminal case. | Rule 1.5(d)(2) flatly prohibits a contingent fee for representing a defendant in a criminal case, so a $75,000 fee payable only on acquittal is impermissible regardless of its reasonableness. A signed agreement or a prosecutor's approval cannot cure a fee the rule forbids outright. |
| 44 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.5, Comment 6 | The domestic-relations prohibition does not extend to recovery of post-judgment balances already due. | Rule 1.5(d)(1) bars contingent fees that depend on securing a divorce or on the amount of support, but Comment 6 explains that the prohibition does not reach collection of post-judgment balances already due under an existing order. Recovering three years of unpaid support for a reasonable percentage of the recovery is therefore permissible. |
| 45 | D | ABA Model Rule 8.4(c) | Dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or misrepresentation may be professional misconduct outside client work. | Rule 8.4(c) makes it professional misconduct to engage in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit or misrepresentation, and the rule is not limited to client matters or the practice of law. Fabricating income records on a personal mortgage application is such conduct, and discipline does not wait for a criminal conviction or a client loss. |
| 46 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.6(b)(6), Comment 15 | A lawyer may comply with a court order after asserting appropriate claims and should disclose only what is required. | Rule 1.6(b)(6) permits disclosure to comply with a court order, and Comment 15 explains that after asserting nonfrivolous privilege and confidentiality objections and losing, the lawyer may comply but should reveal only what the order requires. Confidentiality does not trump an enforceable order, and the order does not license public release of the whole file. |
| 47 | C | ABA Model Rule 8.3(c) | The reporting duty does not require disclosure of information protected by Rule 1.6. | Rule 8.3(c) provides that the reporting duty does not require disclosure of information protected by Rule 1.6, so because the client learned of the theft in confidence and refuses to permit disclosure, the firm must not report. The conduct plainly concerns honesty, but confidentiality controls unless the client consents or another exception applies. |
| 48 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.8(b) | A lawyer may not use representation information to a client's disadvantage without informed consent or other authorization. | Rule 1.8(b) prohibits using information relating to the representation to the client's disadvantage without informed consent, and buying the neighboring parcel to profit from the client's secret expansion plan is exactly that kind of use. The rule is violated by use alone, without any disclosure, and it does not depend on evidentiary privilege. |
| 49 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.7(a), (b) | Direct adversity to a current client creates a conflict even in an unrelated matter. | Rule 1.7(a) treats suing a current client as direct adversity even in a completely unrelated matter, so the firm cannot take the lease dispute against Alpha Foods without Alpha's informed consent confirmed in writing under Rule 1.7(b). An ethical wall addresses confidential information, not loyalty, and the substantial-relationship test belongs to former-client analysis. |
| 50 | D | ABA Model Rule 1.7(b)(3) | One lawyer cannot represent current clients asserting claims against each other in the same litigation. | Rule 1.7(b)(3) makes a conflict nonconsentable when the representation involves asserting a claim by one client against another client represented by the same lawyer in the same litigation, so the driver and passenger cannot both be kept once they sue each other. Their written consent cannot cure it, and their original alignment no longer controls. |
| 51 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.7, Comment 22 | A specific advance waiver by a sophisticated client may provide informed consent to a future consentable conflict. | Rule 1.7, Comment 22 recognizes that an advance waiver can constitute informed consent when it specifically identifies the types of future conflicts and the client, especially a sophisticated one advised by independent counsel, understands the material risks. This waiver did that, so it is not ineffective merely because it predates the conflict; a vague general waiver would fare differently. |
| 52 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.10(a)(1) | A personal-interest conflict is not imputed without a significant risk of materially limiting the remaining lawyers. | Rule 1.10(a)(1) does not impute a conflict based on a lawyer's personal interest when there is no significant risk that it will materially limit the representation by the other lawyers in the firm. The sibling relationship is personal to the first lawyer, so the second lawyer may proceed without the opposing party's consent. |
| 53 | D | ABA Model Rule 1.9(b) | A lawyer moving firms is not personally barred by a former-firm matter without protected information material to it. | Rule 1.9(b) bars a lawyer whose former firm represented an adverse client only if the lawyer acquired protected information material to the matter, and the associate never worked on, discussed, or learned anything about the plaintiff's case. There is no irrebuttable presumption and no need for the plaintiff's consent on these facts. |
| 54 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.8(d) | A lawyer may not negotiate media or literary rights based substantially on representation information before the matter concludes. | Rule 1.8(d) prohibits a lawyer, before the conclusion of the representation, from negotiating an agreement giving the lawyer literary or media rights based substantially on information relating to the representation. Taking documentary rights in lieu of fees during the criminal case is precisely that; the ban is about timing and the source of the rights, not a permanent prohibition. |
| 55 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.8(e)(3) | The rule permits modest basic-needs gifts to indigent pro bono clients when its anti-inducement and other safeguards are met. | Rule 1.8(e)(3) permits a lawyer representing an indigent client pro bono to provide modest gifts for food and other basic living expenses, provided the lawyer does not promise or advertise the assistance, use it to induce the client to stay, or seek repayment. The grocery card met each safeguard, so it is not prohibited financial assistance. |
| 56 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.12(a) | A former third-party neutral may represent a party in the same matter only with every party's informed written consent. | Rule 1.12(a) permits a former mediator to represent a party in the same matter when all parties to the proceeding give informed consent confirmed in writing, which has happened here. Consent from the new client alone would not suffice, and a court appointment is not required. |
| 57 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.13(f) | An organization lawyer must clarify the client's identity when a constituent's interests may be adverse. | Rule 1.13(f) requires a lawyer dealing with an organization's constituent to explain that the organization is the client when the organization's interests may be adverse to the constituent's. Because the employee believes the firm represents him and his exposure may diverge from the company's, the lawyer must give that clarification and suggest separate counsel before continuing. |
| 58 | D | ABA Model Rule 1.14(b), (c) | A lawyer may take reasonably necessary protective action and make narrowly limited disclosures for a client at risk. | Rule 1.14(b) permits reasonably necessary protective action, including consulting a protective agency or seeking a guardian, when a client with diminished capacity faces substantial financial harm and cannot protect herself, and Rule 1.14(c) authorizes revealing information only to the extent reasonably necessary to do so. Diminished capacity neither ends the relationship nor binds the lawyer to every instruction. |
| 59 | C | ABA Model Rule 1.16(a)(2) | Withdrawal is mandatory when the lawyer's condition materially impairs the representation. | Rule 1.16(a)(2) makes withdrawal mandatory when the lawyer's physical or mental condition materially impairs the ability to represent the client, and the medical assessments and work errors establish that impairment. The firm cannot wait for a client complaint or a disciplinary order while the partner keeps active matters. |
| 60 | B | ABA Model Rule 1.16(c) | A lawyer must continue when a tribunal orders continued representation despite otherwise valid grounds to withdraw. | Rule 1.16(c) requires a lawyer to continue the representation when ordered to do so by a tribunal, even when the lawyer otherwise has good cause to terminate, such as being discharged. The lawyer continues through the verdict while protecting the client's interests; a new engagement letter is not needed and ignoring the order is not an option. |
| 61 | A | ABA Model Rule 1.17 | A sale is permitted when the entire practice or practice area is sold with cessation, notice, choice, and fee protections. | Rule 1.17 permits selling an entire practice or an entire area of practice when the seller ceases that practice, each client receives written notice of the sale, the right to retain other counsel or take the file, and the 90-day deemed-consent rule, and fees are not increased by the sale. Selling the whole immigration practice on those terms qualifies; the seller need not leave all other practice areas. |
| 62 | D | ABA Model Rule 2.1 | Candid legal advice may include relevant moral, economic, social, and political considerations. | Rule 2.1 requires candid advice and expressly permits a lawyer to refer not only to the law but to moral, economic, social and political factors relevant to the client's situation. Discussing the human consequences of the plant closure alongside the legal analysis was proper and does not require a financial-adviser license. |
| 63 | C | ABA Model Rule 3.2 | A lawyer must make reasonable efforts to expedite litigation consistent with client interests. | Rule 3.2 requires reasonable efforts to expedite litigation consistent with the client's interests, and its comment makes clear that delay serving no substantial purpose other than to frustrate or burden the opponent is improper. Repeated extensions requested solely to exhaust a weaker opponent fall on the wrong side of that line, so the firm must refuse. |
| 64 | A | ABA Model Rule 3.3(a)(3) | A lawyer may refuse evidence reasonably believed false, except a criminal defendant's own testimony. | Rule 3.3(a)(3) permits a lawyer to refuse to offer evidence, other than the testimony of a criminal defendant, that the lawyer reasonably believes is false, so the partner may decline to call the distrusted nonclient witness. A reasonable belief is enough to refuse; it does not require reporting the witness to the court or waiting for an admission. |
| 65 | B | ABA Model Rule 3.4(b), Comment 3 | Reasonable expert compensation is allowed, but payment may not be contingent on the outcome. | Rule 3.4(b) and Comment 3 allow reasonable compensation of an expert for professional services but prohibit making the expert's fee contingent on the outcome of the case, so the $75,000-only-if-we-win proposal must be rejected. The prohibition is not limited to fact witnesses, and experts may of course be paid on permissible terms. |
| 66 | D | ABA Model Rule 3.4(f) | A request to refrain from volunteering information may be made to a client's employee when that person's interests will not be harmed. | Rule 3.4(f) permits a lawyer to ask a client's employee or agent to refrain from voluntarily giving information to the other side when the lawyer reasonably believes the person's interests will not be adversely affected. The logistics employee meets both conditions; the exception is not limited to relatives, and it never authorizes defying a subpoena. |
| 67 | C | ABA Model Rule 3.5(b); ABA Formal Opinion 466 | Passive review of public juror information is generally not a communication. | Rule 3.5(b) prohibits ex parte communication with a juror, and ABA Formal Opinion 466 concludes that passively viewing a juror's public social-media page, with no friend request, message or platform notification, is not a communication. The research itself was therefore permissible, though any contact or notification would change the analysis. |
| 68 | A | ABA Model Rule 3.6(c) | A narrowly limited reply may mitigate substantial undue prejudice caused by recent publicity from another source. | Rule 3.6(c) allows a lawyer to make a statement that a reasonable lawyer would believe necessary to protect the client from the substantial undue prejudicial effect of recent publicity not initiated by the lawyer or client, limited to information necessary to mitigate it. The response must stay narrow; the opponent's statement does not open the door to discussing all the evidence, and no judicial pre-approval is required. |
| 69 | B | ABA Model Rule 3.8(e) | A prosecutor may subpoena a lawyer when the evidence is unprivileged, essential, and unavailable by a feasible alternative. | Rule 3.8(e) permits a prosecutor to subpoena a lawyer for evidence about a client when the prosecutor reasonably believes the information is not privileged, is essential to the investigation, and has no feasible alternative source. All three conditions are met, so the subpoena may issue; the rule restricts lawyer subpoenas rather than banning them. |
| 70 | D | ABA Model Rule 3.8(d) | A prosecutor must timely disclose information tending to negate guilt or mitigate the offense. | Rule 3.8(d) requires a prosecutor to make timely disclosure of all evidence or information known to the prosecutor that tends to negate the guilt of the accused, and footage showing someone else leaving the scene qualifies. The duty does not depend on the prosecutor's belief that the verdict would change, on a defense request, or on a prior admissibility ruling. |
| 71 | C | ABA Model Rule 4.1(a) | A lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of material fact during negotiation. | Rule 4.1(a) prohibits knowingly making a false statement of material fact to a third person, and the amount of available insurance is a material fact rather than negotiating puffery. Stating a $100,000 limit while knowing of $1 million in coverage is a false statement, and client authorization cannot make it permissible. |
| 72 | A | ABA Model Rule 4.3 | A lawyer must correct an unrepresented person's misunderstanding and avoid conflicting legal advice beyond recommending counsel. | Rule 4.3 requires a lawyer who knows or should know that an unrepresented person misunderstands the lawyer's role to make reasonable efforts to correct it, and bars giving her legal advice beyond recommending independent counsel when her interests may conflict with the client's. | 73 | B | ABA Model Rule 4.4(b) | A lawyer who knows a document was inadvertently sent must promptly notify the sender. | Rule 4.4(b) requires a lawyer who receives a document relating to the representation and knows or reasonably should know it was inadvertently sent to notify the sender promptly. | 74 | D | ABA Model Rule 5.4(a) | A percentage of legal fees ordinarily may not be shared with a nonlawyer marketing company. | Rule 5.4(a) prohibits sharing legal fees with a nonlawyer, and paying a marketing company 5% of fees collected from the clients it generates is fee sharing however the arrangement is labeled, since none of the rule's exceptions applies.
