# LawScape UK SQE Ethics Email Pack

## Instructions

Assume you are a trainee solicitor or newly qualified solicitor receiving each email from a colleague in an England and Wales practice. Select the single best response under the SRA Principles, the SRA Code of Conduct for Solicitors, RELs, RFLs and RSLs, the SRA Code of Conduct for Firms, the SRA Accounts Rules, or the financial-crime rules identified in the question.

These 28 questions are original LawScape educational hypotheticals. They use the practical topic coverage of the supplied Quizlet PDF and the SQE1 Prep ethics revision guide, together with the single-best-answer approach illustrated by the SRA/Kaplan SQE1 sample questions. They are not official SQE questions and do not reproduce the wording of the source questions.

The SRA pages can change, and SQE1 applies a law-and-practice cut-off date. Always check the version applicable to your assessment window.

## 1. The Black Cab Cross-Examination

> **From:** Imogen Fairweather, London Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Black cab collision - one question too far?
>
> Morning from a rain-soaked Fleet Street. Our claimant originally told us that the black-cab driver had both hands on the wheel immediately before the collision. Today, outside court, he asked me to cross-examine the driver on the basis that she was typing a message at the time. He admits that he never saw a phone.
>
> There are several genuine issues about speed and visibility that I can properly challenge. May I also put the phone allegation so the judge can decide whether it has legs?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes. A solicitor may put any allegation the client instructs, provided it is phrased as a question.
- **B.** No. Do not put the phone allegation, but cross-examine on the genuine issues that remain properly arguable.
- **C.** Yes. The allegation is permissible because cross-examination is not evidence given by the solicitor.
- **D.** No. The solicitor must stop acting immediately and tell the court what the client said in confidence.

## 2. The Missing Pub Conviction

> **From:** Oliver Wren, London Crime Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Sentencing list - a rather important omission
>
> Our client is due to be sentenced at Southwark Crown Court after pleading guilty to assault. The court's list of antecedents omits last year's conviction for assaulting a pub landlord. The judge has asked whether the list is complete.
>
> The client has refused permission to correct it. He says the omission is the court clerk's problem and has instructed me to confirm that the list is accurate. What is the proper course?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Confirm the list because the solicitor did not prepare it.
- **B.** Reveal the omitted conviction despite the client's refusal because candour always displaces confidentiality.
- **C.** Say nothing about the accuracy of the list and continue the mitigation as though no question was asked.
- **D.** Refuse to mislead the court, try to persuade the client to permit correction, and cease acting if the client persists without revealing the confidence.

## 3. The Unhelpful Cricket-Club Witness

> **From:** Poppy Ashdown, Birmingham Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Witness from the cricket pavilion
>
> I interviewed a groundskeeper who saw our client near the village cricket pavilion at the relevant time. Unfortunately, her honest account undermines the defence. We have not served a witness statement from her, and neither the prosecution nor the court knows that we spoke to her.
>
> The client wants me to ask her to "remember the evening more helpfully." Must we call her, disclose her account, or do something else?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Do not influence her evidence; on these facts there is no general duty to call her or volunteer her account.
- **B.** Call her because every witness interviewed by the defence must give evidence.
- **C.** Send her account to the prosecution because any evidence adverse to a defendant must be volunteered.
- **D.** Ask her to reconsider the account, provided no money or other benefit is offered.

## 4. The Biscuit-Tin Authority

> **From:** Harriet Cole, London Disputes Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Bad case discovered under the biscuit tin
>
> Opposing counsel has overlooked a Court of Appeal decision that is directly relevant and likely to have a material effect on tomorrow's application. It cuts against our construction of the contract, although I think it can be distinguished.
>
> The client says we should keep quiet because the other side has had ample time to do its homework. What should go into the skeleton argument?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Omit the case because only binding Supreme Court authorities must be disclosed.
- **B.** Mention it only if the judge asks whether contrary authority exists.
- **C.** Draw the court's attention to the case and explain why it should be distinguished.
- **D.** Send the case privately to opposing counsel but leave it out of the court materials.

## 5. Shares in the Tea-Robot Company

> **From:** Imogen Fairweather, London Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Due diligence on the automatic tea trolley
>
> Our client wants us to conduct due diligence on Britannia BrewBot Ltd before investing. I own a sizeable shareholding in BrewBot, which I bought after its machine produced an excellent cuppa at a legal-tech fair. I would benefit if the investment proceeds.
>
> The client knows about my shares, is commercially sophisticated, and is willing to consent in writing. Can I lead the due diligence?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes, because a sophisticated client can consent to any conflict in writing.
- **B.** No. The financial interest creates an own-interest conflict or significant risk of one, and consent does not create an exception.
- **C.** Yes, if another partner reviews the final report before it is sent.
- **D.** No, but only because the investment concerns a technology company.

## 6. The Canal-Boat Co-Founders

> **From:** Rhys Morgan, Cardiff Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Two founders, one narrowboat business
>
> Two clients want to buy and operate a canal-boat holiday company together. They share a clear purpose and have a strong consensus on the price, ownership percentages, funding, and management plan. A limited point remains on which their interests could diverge.
>
> Both understand the risk, have given informed consent evidenced in writing, and agree to appropriate confidentiality safeguards. We are satisfied it is reasonable and in each client's best interests for us to act. May we?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A firm can never act for two clients on the same transaction.
- **B.** Yes, because oral consent would have been sufficient once their commercial objectives aligned.
- **C.** No. The competing-for-the-same-objective exception is the only exception available.
- **D.** Yes. The substantially-common-interest exception can apply because all required conditions are met.

## 7. The Cotswold Cottage Gift

> **From:** Beatrice Bell, Bristol Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Father, daughter and the Cotswold cottage
>
> A father is selling a cottage to his adult daughter at a price fixed after independent valuation. Both have already agreed every relevant term, all material information may be shared openly, neither is vulnerable, and no substantive negotiation is expected. Each has been advised about separate representation but wants our firm to document the agreed transaction.
>
> After assessing the matter, we see no significant risk that our duties will conflict and believe acting is in both clients' best interests. Is joint representation automatically barred?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Acting is not automatically barred where the facts show no significant conflict risk, but the position must be kept under review.
- **B.** Yes. A solicitor must never act for both transferor and transferee, even where every term is settled.
- **C.** No, because family members are outside the conflict rules.
- **D.** Yes, unless the daughter pays full market value rather than receiving any gift element.

## 8. The Forgotten Mayfair Client

> **From:** Sanjay Patel, London Conflicts Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Former Mayfair client and the locked-room team
>
> We hold confidential information from a former hotel client. A new client wants us to pursue an adverse claim in which that information is material. The former client will not consent.
>
> Before accepting the matter, the firm created established structural separation: different fee earners, access controls, segregated records, and monitoring. The measures leave no real risk that the former client's information will be disclosed. May the screened team act?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. Only the former client's written consent can ever permit the firm to act.
- **B.** Yes, because confidentiality ends when the retainer ends.
- **C.** Yes. Effective measures resulting in no real risk of disclosure can satisfy the alternative in paragraph 6.5.
- **D.** No, unless the new client also waives its right to receive all material information.

## 9. The Two Prices for a Bath Flat

> **From:** Beatrice Bell, Bristol Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Buyer, lender and two different prices
>
> We act for a buyer and her mortgage lender on a Bath flat. The buyer has privately revealed that the true purchase price is £40,000 lower than the price stated in the lender's papers. The difference is plainly material to the lender.
>
> The buyer refuses permission to tell the lender and insists that we complete before the Friday train home. What should we do?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Complete because the buyer's confidentiality always permits continued representation.
- **B.** Seek consent to disclose; if it is refused, do not mislead the lender and cease acting as required without revealing the confidential reason.
- **C.** Tell the lender immediately because the duty of disclosure always overrides the buyer's confidentiality.
- **D.** Amend the price in the lender's papers without explaining why.

## 11. The Ten-O'Clock Deposit Promise

> **From:** Imogen Fairweather, London Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** "Promise" made before the kettle boiled
>
> During a tense conveyancing call, our solicitor told an unrepresented seller: "My firm promises to send the £20,000 deposit by 10 am tomorrow." She never used the word "undertaking," but the seller relied on the statement and stood down another buyer.
>
> The accounts team now says the transfer will be inconvenient. Can we treat the statement as ordinary negotiation?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** No. A relied-upon promise can be an undertaking without using that label and must be performed within the agreed time.
- **B.** Yes, because an undertaking must contain the word "undertake."
- **C.** Yes, because oral undertakings are never binding.
- **D.** No, but only the individual solicitor has regulatory responsibility for performance.

## 12. Repairs Before the Morning Train

> **From:** Beatrice Bell, Bristol Office Partner
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Repairs promised; seller now in Cornwall
>
> Completion is tomorrow. To stop the buyer withdrawing, our solicitor promised the buyer's solicitor that the seller would finish specified repairs before completion. The seller had never authorised that promise and is now unreachable on a walking holiday in Cornwall.
>
> What is our professional-conduct position?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** There is no undertaking because the seller did not authorise it.
- **B.** The undertaking disappears automatically because performance depends on a third party.
- **C.** The promise may bind as an undertaking; the firm should urgently seek release or an agreed alternative and remains exposed if it is not performed.
- **D.** The solicitor may substitute reasonable endeavours for the promised result without consent.

## 14. The Complaint Under the Tea Tray

## 17. The Trainee and the Mayfair Mansion

## 18. The Missed Deadline Before Afternoon Tea

> **From:** Harriet Cole, London Disputes Partner

> Our team missed a limitation deadline. The client may have lost a valuable claim and may also have a negligence claim against the firm. A partner suggests saying only that "procedural complications arose," then advising the client ourselves to accept a modest refund before our insurer hears about it.

## 24. The SAR and the Coded Voicemail

> **From:** Sanjay Patel, London Office Partner

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Yes, because a solicitor must always tell the client every reason for delay.

- **C.** No, but the firm should instead invent a false reason for the delay.
- **D.** No. Disclosing the SAR or related information in a way likely to prejudice an investigation risks the tipping-off offence; communications must be truthful and carefully managed.

## 28. The COFA and the Empty Client Account

> **From:** Daisy Pembroke, London Office COFA
> **To:** You
> **Subject:** Shortfall discovered before the bank holiday
>
> I have discovered a substantial client-account shortfall caused by repeated unauthorised withdrawals. The managing partner wants me to wait until after the bank holiday so the firm can quietly replace the money. The facts are capable of amounting to a serious breach of the Accounts Rules.
>
> What must I do as COFA?

**Which response should the associate give?**

- **A.** Wait, because replacing the money before any client complains removes the breach.
- **B.** Report only to the COLP, who has exclusive responsibility for Accounts Rules breaches.
- **C.** Resign immediately; resignation alone satisfies every regulatory duty.
- **D.** Take all reasonable steps to secure Accounts Rules compliance and prompt correction, and ensure the serious matter is reported promptly to the SRA.

# Answer Key and Tested Rules

| Question | Answer | Primary authority | Core reason | Explanation |
|---:|:---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | B | SRA Principles 1, 2, 4 and 5; Solicitors Code 1.4 and 2.4 | A solicitor must not mislead and may put only properly arguable assertions, while still advancing genuine issues in the client's case. | Solicitors Code 1.4 and 2.4 bar misleading the court and allow you to put only assertions that are properly arguable, and Principles 1, 2, 4 and 5 reinforce that duty; a phone allegation the client admits he never saw has no proper basis. The genuine points on speed and visibility can and should still be pursued, so refusing the phone question does not mean ceasing to act or disclosing what the client said. |
| 2 | D | Solicitors Code 1.4 and 6.3; SRA Principles 1 and 7 | The solicitor cannot confirm a known inaccuracy or reveal the confidence without authority; if the client refuses correction, withdrawal may be required. | Solicitors Code 1.4 prohibits misleading the court, so you cannot confirm a list you know is incomplete, while Code 6.3 and Principle 7 prevent you from revealing the omitted conviction without the client's authority. The proper path is to try to persuade the client to allow correction and, if he refuses and the court would otherwise be misled, to cease acting without disclosing the confidence. |
| 3 | A | Solicitors Code 1.4 and 2.2 | A solicitor must not influence the substance of evidence, but there is no general duty on these facts to call an adverse defence witness or volunteer the account. | Solicitors Code 2.2 forbids seeking to influence the substance of evidence, so the request to help the groundskeeper "remember more helpfully" must be refused even without any inducement. Code 1.4 is not engaged by silence: the defence has no general duty to call an adverse witness or to volunteer an unserved account to the prosecution or the court. |
| 4 | C | Solicitors Code 2.7 | The solicitor must draw the court's attention to relevant authority likely to have a material effect and may then argue that it is distinguishable. | Solicitors Code 2.7 requires you to draw the court's attention to relevant cases and provisions likely to have a material effect on the outcome, which this Court of Appeal decision is. The client's wish to stay quiet and the other side's poor research do not displace the duty; cite the case in the skeleton and argue that it is distinguishable. |
| 5 | B | Solicitors Code 6.1; SRA conflicts guidance | A solicitor's financial interest creates an own-interest conflict or significant risk; the prohibition has no consent exception. | Solicitors Code 6.1 prohibits acting where there is an own-interest conflict or a significant risk of one, and a partner's sizeable shareholding in the target of the client's due diligence is such a risk. Unlike a client conflict under Code 6.2, the own-interest prohibition has no consent exception, so the client's sophisticated written consent and a second-partner review cannot cure it. |
| 6 | D | Solicitors Code 6.2(a) | A substantially common interest can permit acting where all clients give informed written consent, safeguards are used where appropriate, and acting is reasonable. | Solicitors Code 6.2(a) allows acting for both founders because they have a substantially common interest, both have given informed consent evidenced in writing, appropriate safeguards are in place, and the firm is satisfied that it is reasonable to act. Written evidence of consent is a condition of the exception, and the competing-for-the-same-objective limb in Code 6.2(b) is a separate, alternative route. |
| 7 | A | Solicitors Code 6.2; SRA conflicts guidance | Acting for both sides is not automatically barred if the settled facts create no significant conflict risk, information is openly shared, and acting remains in each client's best interests. | Solicitors Code 6.2 bars acting for both parties only where there is a conflict or a significant risk of one; where every term is already settled after independent valuation, information is shared openly, neither party is vulnerable and no negotiation is expected, the SRA conflicts guidance accepts that acting may be proper. The position must be kept under review, and family status does not take the parties outside the rules. |
| 8 | C | Solicitors Code 6.3 and 6.5(a) | Former-client confidentiality continues, but effective measures that leave no real risk of disclosure can permit the adverse representation. | Solicitors Code 6.3 keeps the former hotel client's information confidential, but Code 6.5(a) permits acting for a client with an adverse interest where effective measures have been taken so that there is no real risk of disclosure. The firm's structural separation satisfies that alternative, so the former client's refusal to consent under Code 6.5(b) is not fatal; confidentiality does not end with the retainer. |
| 9 | B | Solicitors Code 1.4, 6.3 and 6.4 | The material-information duty cannot be performed by breaching the buyer's confidence; consent should be sought and refusal may require the firm to stop acting. | Solicitors Code 6.4 requires you to tell the lender about the true price, but Code 6.3 prohibits disclosing the buyer's confidence without consent, and Code 1.4 prohibits misleading the lender. When the buyer refuses consent the duties cannot both be met, so the firm must stop acting for the lender without revealing why; completing or silently amending the papers would mislead. |
| 10 | D | Solicitors Code 4.1 and 5.1 | A financial benefit arising from instructions must be disclosed and accounted to the client unless the client agrees otherwise. | Solicitors Code 4.1 and 5.1 require you to inform the client of any financial benefit arising from their instructions and to account for it to the client unless the client has agreed otherwise. The surveyor's £150 is such a benefit even though it was unsolicited and small, so the firm must disclose it rather than treat it as a windfall or quietly keep it. |
| 11 | A | Solicitors Code 1.3; SRA Glossary definition of undertaking | The substance and reasonable reliance matter, not the label; an undertaking must be performed within the agreed time. | Under the SRA Glossary an undertaking is a statement made to someone who reasonably relies on it that you or your firm will do something, and Solicitors Code 1.3 requires it to be performed within the agreed time. The seller stood down another buyer in reliance on the 10 am promise, so the missing word "undertaking" and the inconvenience to accounts do not matter; the obligation binds the firm, not just the individual. |
| 12 | C | Solicitors Code 1.3; SRA undertakings guidance | A solicitor should not promise a result outside the firm's control; an undertaking remains a regulatory obligation unless released or varied by agreement. | Solicitors Code 1.3 treats a promise relied on by the buyer's solicitor as an undertaking, and the SRA undertakings guidance warns against promising outcomes outside the firm's control; the seller's lack of authorisation does not release the firm from what its solicitor said. The firm should urgently seek a release or an agreed variation and remains exposed if the repairs are not done; it cannot unilaterally downgrade the promise to reasonable endeavours. |
| 13 | B | Solicitors Code 8.3 | The client's complaints rights and process must be communicated in writing at the time of engagement. | Solicitors Code 8.3 requires that clients be told in writing, at the time of engagement, of their right to complain, how and to whom, and of their right to go to the Legal Ombudsman. Accurate oral information does not satisfy the writing requirement, and the later complaint being unfounded does not undo the breach. |
| 14 | D | Solicitors Code 8.5 | Every complaint must be dealt with promptly, fairly, and free of charge, regardless of the eventual merits. | Solicitors Code 8.5 requires every complaint to be dealt with promptly, fairly and free of charge, and leaving the letter untouched for six weeks fails the promptness requirement regardless of how the complaint is later resolved. The Legal Ombudsman's rejection of the underlying criticism does not retrospectively excuse the firm's handling of it. |
| 15 | C | Solicitors Code 8.9 | A neutral invited talk may be proper, but targeted unsolicited approaches to advertise legal services to non-clients are prohibited. | Solicitors Code 8.9 prohibits unsolicited approaches to members of the public to advertise legal services, so handing out firm adverts and taking instant instructions from residents who are not clients breaches the Code even though the invited, even-handed talk itself was proper. The manager's invitation and accurate pricing do not convert targeted solicitation into permitted publicity. |
| 16 | A | Solicitors Code 6.1; SRA drafting-and-preparation-of-wills guidance | A significant gift to the drafter calls for independent advice and a continuing assessment of whether an own-interest conflict requires the solicitor to cease acting. | A significant legacy to the drafting solicitor engages Solicitors Code 6.1 and the SRA guidance on drafting wills, which expects the client to take independent advice and the solicitor to assess whether an own-interest conflict or significant risk remains. The solicitor may continue only if, after that advice, impartial action is possible; a referral list alone or drafting straight away because the client suggested it is insufficient, while deleting the gift overrides her instructions. |
| 17 | A | Solicitors Code 3.2, 3.3, 3.5 and 3.6; Firms Code 4.3 and 4.4 | The service must be competent and effectively supervised; delegation does not remove the supervisor's or firm's accountability. | Solicitors Code 3.2 to 3.6 require a competent and timely service with effective supervision of work done by others, and Firms Code 4.3 and 4.4 place the same duty on the firm. Giving a first-week trainee sole conduct of a £12 million purchase with complex source-of-funds issues and a single post-exchange review fails those requirements, and delegation does not shift accountability onto the trainee. |
| 18 | C | Solicitors Code 6.1 and 7.11; SRA putting-matters-right guidance | The firm must explain the error and impact promptly, try to put matters right, and avoid advising where its own liability creates a conflict. | Solicitors Code 7.11 requires the firm to be open and honest with the client about the missed limitation period and its likely impact, and the SRA putting-matters-right guidance expects prompt remedial steps and insurer notification. Because the firm's own negligence liability conflicts with the client's interest, Code 6.1 means the client must be directed to independent advice rather than steered by the firm toward a modest refund. |
| 19 | B | Solicitors Code 7.7 and 7.12; Firms Code 9.1 | Facts reasonably believed capable of amounting to a serious breach must be reported promptly; reporting appropriately through the COLP can satisfy the individual duty. | Solicitors Code 7.7 requires promptly reporting facts reasonably believed capable of amounting to a serious breach, and the deliberate, repeated billing of personal trips as hearing expenses qualifies; Code 7.12 and Firms Code 9.1 allow that duty to be satisfied by reporting through the COLP on the understanding that the COLP will report to the SRA. Refunds, resignation and the absence of a client request do not remove the duty, and no dishonesty finding is required first. |
| 20 | D | SRA Accounts Rules 4.2 and 6.1 | Misallocated or improperly held client money must be corrected promptly and cannot be appropriated for an unrelated bill. | SRA Accounts Rule 6.1 requires any breach to be corrected promptly on discovery, and Rule 4.2 ensures client money is used only for that client's matter, so the £9,000 wrongly credited to the ledger must be returned or reallocated to its true owner. It cannot be transferred to business account to clear an unrelated bill, however neat the arithmetic. |
| 21 | A | SRA Accounts Rules 4.3 | Before transferring client money for costs, the firm must give written notification and transfer only the specific notified sum held for that client. | SRA Accounts Rule 4.3 permits transferring client money for costs only after a bill or other written notification has been given to the client, and only for the specific sum notified and covered by that client's funds. An estimated Friday transfer with the bill to follow on Monday reverses that sequence; earned costs do not stop being client money until the notification is sent. |
| 22 | C | SRA Accounts Rules 3.3 | A client account must not be used as a banking facility; transactions must relate to regulated services delivered by the firm. | SRA Accounts Rule 3.3 prohibits using a client account to provide banking facilities, and receiving £300,000 from a cousin to pass to an auction house on a purchase handled by another firm is not connected to any regulated service the firm is delivering. Being a long-standing client, running identity checks, or shortening the holding period does not change that. |
| 23 | B | POCA 2002 ss.330-336; SRA SAR warning notice | Suspicion arising in regulated work triggers the reporting framework; the fee earner must use the firm's process, avoid tipping off, and not proceed unlawfully. | Suspicion arising in regulated work triggers POCA 2002 ss.330 to 336, so the fee earner must report promptly to the firm's nominated officer or MLRO, follow the SAR and defence-against-money-laundering process, avoid tipping off, and not proceed unlawfully. Avoiding the phrase "suspicious activity report" does not help, and the firm must not invent a false reason either; communications must be truthful but carefully managed. |
